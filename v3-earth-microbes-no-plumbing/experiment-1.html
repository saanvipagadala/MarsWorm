Experiment 1: does Azospira clean dirt, and not just water?

Stage 1 of the design asks one bacterium to destroy the perchlorate in damp Martian dirt, fed nothing but hydrogen. Every published result for this bacterium is from water in a stirred tank. Nobody has run it in regolith. This experiment asks whether it works on grains at all, and it is first because if the answer is no, version 3 has no stage 1 and nothing after it.

What we would be asking of you
The question Does Azospira suillum PS destroy perchlorate while stuck to damp regolith grains, with hydrogen as its only fuel and nothing to build new cells from?
Bench space 12 jars on about half a metre of shelf
How long Four weeks
Biosafety BSL-1, plus a few grams of sodium perchlorate, an oxidiser
From the lab Sealed jars or a hydrogen line, a scale to 0.01 g, an ion meter or a spectrophotometer, and somewhere to lock a small bottle of perchlorate
From us Everything else, listed below
Student on site A short session a week

Why this one first

What we are looking at What it says
What is known This bacterium destroys perchlorate in drinking water plants every day, and the hydrogen-fed version of that reactor is a working technology
Why the gap matters A grain is not a tank. The cell is stuck to a surface, nothing stirs its food towards it, and the hydrogen has to find its own way through wet grit to reach it
What this decides Whether stage 1 exists. Every later stage assumes the dirt has been cleaned in place, with no washing and no plumbing

Design

Four treatments, three jars each, 12 jars.

Jar What is in it
A simulant, damp, perchlorate, bacterium, hydrogen
B simulant, damp, perchlorate, no bacterium, hydrogen
C water, perchlorate, bacterium, hydrogen
D simulant, damp, perchlorate, bacterium, no hydrogen
  • Each control takes the credit away. B says whether the dirt itself destroys perchlorate. C says whether the bacterium can do it here at all, in the medium it is used to. D says whether the hydrogen is what pays for it.
  • Nothing is fed to any jar. No carbon source of any kind goes in. The design needs the bacterium to clean without growing, so the experiment has to be run that way.
  • Perchlorate goes in at Martian strength, about 0.5 percent of the dry weight of the simulant, which is what the Phoenix lander measured.

Somewhere lockable for a small bottle of oxidiser

On the hydrogen. Hydrogen burns in air above about four percent, so the jars are flushed and sealed away from any flame, and the flushing is done by whoever normally handles gas in your lab. Nothing here needs high pressure, and a sealed jar with hydrogen in the headspace is enough.

Setup

On day one Why
1 Weigh the same mass of simulant into each of the nine dirt jars
2 Dissolve the perchlorate in water first, then stir it through the simulant. Jar C gets the same dose in water with no simulant nothing goes in dry and uneven
3 Bring every dirt jar to the same dampness, the feel of a wrung-out cloth
4 Add the bacterium to A, C and D. B gets the same volume of sterile liquid so the only difference is whether it is alive
5 Flush the headspace of A, B and C with hydrogen and seal. D is sealed under nitrogen
6 Dark, room temperature. Note the date

What happens, and when

When What to do
Day 0 Take a sample from every jar for its starting perchlorate. This is the number everything else is measured against
Weekly, four times One sample per jar. Perchlorate, and chloride if the method gives it. Top the hydrogen up and reseal
Day 28 A last full set of samples, and a cell count on the dirt jars to show whether the bacterium held on without growing

What we expect to see

Where to look What we expect
Jar A Perchlorate falling week by week, with chloride rising as it goes. Slower than the water jar, because nothing is stirred
Jars B and D No change. If either falls, the result belongs to the chemistry or to something we did not control, not to the bacterium
Jar C The fastest fall, and the yardstick for how much the dirt costs
What would surprise us Jar A matching jar C. That would mean grains cost nothing, and the design is cheaper than we have assumed
What would end it Jar A flat while jar C falls. The bacterium can do the chemistry, but not where we need it done

One extra jar, for a question stage 1 cannot answer alone

The next bed is started with a scoop of a finished one, so Xanthobacter and Bacillus get carried into dirt that still holds perchlorate. They do not have to work there, only survive until stage 2. One more jar, set up like A but holding all three organisms and counted at the end, answers that while the shelf is already busy.

Recording rules

  • Label by number and keep the key aside until the last samples are measured.
  • Write down what is seen, not what it means. A jar that leaks, dries or gets knocked over gets a note on the day, and the note stays in the report whatever it does to the result.

The sheets, blank and filled in

Spreadsheets, printed and filled in by hand at the bench, then typed up exactly as written. Every column is defined in the codebook. They are empty, because nobody has run this yet.

The sheet What goes in it
jars.csv which jar is which treatment, what went into it, and where it sits on the shelf
readings.csv perchlorate and chloride, once a week, jar by jar. The sheet the answer comes from
care_log.csv the weekly damp check, and whether the hydrogen was topped up
cells.csv cells at the start and the end, which should barely move

The same sheets also exist with invented numbers in them, made by a script so the sheets can be checked before there is real data. This set is the expected outcome: the dirt jars clean, more slowly than the water jar, and the cell counts hardly change. jars · readings · care_log · cells · a dry run, not a result

The analysis reads those sheets and writes a report: charts, a verdict for each question, and its own checks on the data. The rules that decide the verdicts were fixed before any data existed. See what that report looks like, built from the invented sheets, and it says dry run in its own title
