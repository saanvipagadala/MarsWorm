Experiment 3: does Xanthobacter grow in Martian dirt?

Stage 2 asks one bacterium to build the food, the nitrogen and the bodies that turn clean dirt into something a worm can live in, out of nothing but gas. It has been grown on carbon dioxide, nitrogen and hydrogen in a flask, and that is published. It has never been grown in regolith, which is abrasive, salty and alkaline. This experiment asks whether the flask result survives the move into dirt.

What we would be asking of you
The question Does Xanthobacter autotrophicus Py2 grow in damp regolith on gas alone, and does it keep pulling nitrogen out of the air while it does?
Bench space 12 jars on a shelf, or on a shaker if you would rather
How long Five weeks
Biosafety BSL-1. Nothing modified. Hydrogen in a sealed headspace, so no flames
From the lab A gas mix of carbon dioxide, nitrogen and hydrogen, a plate reader or turbidity meter, and a scale to 0.01 g
From us Everything else, listed below
Student on site Short measuring sessions, twice a week

Why it matters

What is known In 2022 a group fed this bacterium nothing but carbon dioxide, nitrogen and hydrogen, with the hydrogen made by splitting water, and it grew. That is a Mars habitat described in three gases
Why the gap matters Three other microbes grown in Mars simulant kept only about a tenth of the growth they manage in ordinary broth. Regolith is salty, alkaline and sharp, and none of that is in a flask
What this decides Whether stage 2 needs anything shipped from Earth. If it only grows with fertiliser added, the design has lost the thing it was built to avoid

Design

Four treatments, three jars each, 12 jars.

Jar What is in it
A simulant, damp, gas mix, bacterium
B standard medium, gas mix, bacterium
C simulant, damp, gas mix, bacterium, fertiliser added
D simulant, damp, gas mix, no bacterium
  • B is the yardstick. It is the published condition, and the gap between A and B is the price of the dirt.
  • C says what the nitrogen fixing is worth. If A and C grow alike, the bacterium is feeding itself nitrogen perfectly well. If C races ahead, nitrogen is the thing holding stage 2 back.
  • D shows what the dirt does on its own, including anything in the simulant that a turbidity reading might mistake for cells.
  • The gas is roughly 70 parts hydrogen, 15 nitrogen, 10 carbon dioxide and 5 oxygen. The oxygen is deliberately low, because the enzyme that catches nitrogen is wrecked by it.

Materials

What How much

The lab already has
Carbon dioxide, nitrogen, hydrogen and air, and a way to mix them
A plate reader, turbidity meter or spectrophotometer for cell counts
An incubator or warm shelf at 30 degrees
A protein assay, or an acetylene reduction setup if you have one for the nitrogen question

On the hydrogen. Hydrogen burns in air above about four percent. The bottles are flushed and sealed by whoever normally handles gas in your lab, away from any flame. Nothing here needs pressure beyond a sealed bottle.

Setup

On day one Why
1 Weigh the same mass of simulant into each of the nine dirt jars
2 Wet every dirt jar with the same volume of salts solution. C gets the nitrogen source, A and D get none so the only difference is the fertiliser
3 Fill B with standard medium to the same liquid volume
4 Add the bacterium to A, B and C. D gets the same volume, sterile
5 Flush every headspace with the gas mix and seal
6 30 degrees, dark. Note the date

What happens, and when

When What to do
Twice a week A small sample from each jar for cells. Flush and reseal the headspace, so no jar runs out of gas
Weekly Note the pH, because regolith drifts alkaline and that alone can stop growth
Day 35 A final cell count, and the nitrogen measurement on every jar

What we expect to see

Jar B Growth, as published. If B fails, the culture or the gas is wrong and nothing else can be read
Jar A Slower growth than B, and we would call anything above a tenth of it a working stage 2
Jar C against A The size of the nitrogen penalty. A small gap is the answer we want
What would surprise us A matching B. That would mean regolith is no harder than a flask, which nothing published so far suggests
What would end it Growth only in C. Stage 2 would then need fertiliser flown from Earth, and version 3 exists to avoid exactly that

Recording rules

  • Label by number and keep the key aside until the last count is taken.
  • Write down what is seen, not what it means. A leak, a dried jar or a missed flush gets a note on the day, and the note stays in the report.

The sheets, blank and filled in

Spreadsheets, printed and filled in by hand at the bench, then typed up exactly as written. Every column is defined in the codebook. They are empty, because nobody has run this yet.

The sheet What goes in it
jars.csv which jar is which treatment, and what went into it
growth.csv the meter reading, twice a week, jar by jar
nitrogen.csv protein and nitrogen at the start and the end
care_log.csv the weekly round, including any seal that looked wrong

The same sheets also exist with invented numbers in them, made by a script so the sheets can be checked before there is real data.
