Stage 2. Enrich it

The poison is gone and the dirt is safe, and it is still dead. This is the stage that makes it alive. Xanthobacter builds itself out of nothing but air and hydrogen, blows the leftover chlorine away as a gas, and dies to become the dead material soil is made of. Bacillus eats what it leaves and cracks the rock open for phosphorus.

Who does the work Xanthobacter, which fixes its own carbon and nitrogen out of the air, and Bacillus megaterium, which frees phosphorus
What happens Carbon and nitrogen are fixed out of the air, the chlorine is turned into a gas and leaves, and the rock is opened up
Out Dirt with dead material in it, fixed nitrogen, and phosphorus and iron a root can reach

Xanthobacter builds itself out of air

  • It needs no food at all. It takes carbon dioxide and nitrogen straight from Mars air, and burns hydrogen for the energy to do it. No sugar, no fertiliser, no light.
  • No light matters more than it sounds. Light does not travel through dirt. A tonne of regolith spread thin enough to light covers 388 square metres. Any microbe that needs light has to live in a tank, and a tank is the thing version 3 is trying to delete.
  • It fixes its own nitrogen. Mars has no fertiliser of any kind, so anything living there either pulls nitrogen out of the air or dies.
  • Then it dies, and that is the point. Soil is dirt with dead things in it. The dead Xanthobacter is most of what turns this bed into soil.

The chlorine leaves here, as a gas

  • This is where the one added gene lives. It is borrowed from a salt marsh plant, and it takes a chloride ion and a methyl group and makes methyl chloride. That boils at minus 24, so in a warm bed it is a gas and it leaves on its own.
  • The gene has to be in Xanthobacter, not in Azospira. Making the gas spends a methyl group every time, and a methyl group is made of carbon. Azospira cannot build carbon out of air and has nothing to spend. Xanthobacter makes its own carbon, so it always has some. The gene goes where the carbon is, and that is why the chlorine comes out in stage 2 rather than stage 1.
  • It is cleared slowly, on purpose. The cell will not do it unless it is made to, and pushing it hard costs a lot of growth. Spread across the whole of stage 2 it costs about three per cent.
Chlorine cleared over What it costs
a rushed stage 12% of the growth
four times as long 3%
eight times as long 2%

The rule is to set the rate so the chlorine finishes when the soil finishes. There is no reason to hurry, and hurrying is the only way to make it expensive.

Bacillus opens the rock

  • Mars has plenty of phosphorus and none of it is reachable. Ten times as much as Earth farm soil, locked inside a hard mineral called apatite. Think of sugar sealed in glass.
  • Bacillus megaterium makes an acid that cracks it open. Gardeners buy it by the bag to do the same job in ordinary flower beds.
  • The same acid frees the iron. Mars is red because its iron is rust, and roots cannot take up rust.
  • It has to eat first, which is why it belongs here. Bacillus cannot build itself out of air. It needs dead material, and there is none until Xanthobacter has grown and died. So the phosphorus arrives after the nitrogen, not before.

Everything multiplies here, and that is what closes the cycle

Stage 1 had nothing living in it, so nothing could breed. Stage 2 is the opposite. Once Xanthobacter has built itself out of the air and begun to die, there is food in the bed, and all three microbes grow on it.

  • Xanthobacter grows first, on nothing but Mars air and hydrogen. It needs no one’s help to get started.
  • Azospira grows on what Xanthobacter leaves behind. It cannot build itself out of air, so it waits for this stage, and then it multiplies properly.
  • Bacillus does the same, and makes its acid while it goes.

So a finished bed carries more of all three than was put into it. The first bed on Mars needs a starter brought from Earth. After that, the beds seed each other.

How the next bed is started

Nothing is extracted. There is no way to filter bacteria out of dirt, and no need to. A scoop of this bed is mixed into the next one, and the microbes come with it. That is how a gardener starts a compost heap, with a shovelful of the last one.

The scoop is taken at the end of stage 2, before the worms go in. That matters. The dirt is at its richest in all three microbes then, and it has no worms in it yet, so none are carried into a bed that still has perchlorate in it.

With more than one bed running, they stagger. A bed finishing stage 2 hands a scoop to a bed about to start, and takes its own worms next. No bed ever has to wait for another, and nothing is stored.

One thing here is untested, and it is worth saying. The scoop carries Xanthobacter and Bacillus into a bed that is still full of perchlorate, and neither has ever met perchlorate. They do not have to work in stage 1, only survive it. Whether they do is a bench question, and it is part of experiment 1.

Why it matters

  • Stage 1 made the dirt safe. This is the stage that makes it fertile. Dead material, nitrogen, phosphorus and iron all arrive here, and the last of the chlorine leaves.
