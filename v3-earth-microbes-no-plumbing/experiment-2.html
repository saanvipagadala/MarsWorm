Experiment 2: how much perchlorate can an earthworm stand?

Stage 3 puts earthworms into dirt that stage 1 has cleaned. Nobody knows how clean that has to be. There is no published number for how much perchlorate an earthworm will tolerate before it stops breeding. This experiment produces that number. It is the cheapest thing in the project, and it says when stage 1 is allowed to stop.

What we would be asking of you
The question At what concentration of perchlorate do Eisenia fetida stop reproducing, and at what concentration do they die?
Bench space 18 takeaway boxes on about half a metre of shelf
How long Nine weeks, mostly waiting
Biosafety BSL-1. No genetic modification, no vertebrates
From the lab Shelf space at room temperature, a scale to 0.01 g, and somewhere to lock a small bottle of sodium perchlorate
From us Everything else, listed below
Student on site Two short sessions, at week two and week nine

The answer serves two designs. Version 2 asks for the same experiment, because it has the same worms at the end of it. The bench work is identical. What differs is what the number is used for: version 2 sizes a wash with it, and version 3 has no wash, so it decides how long the bacterium in stage 1 has to keep going.

Why it matters here

What is known Earthworms die above about 2,000 millionths by weight. They stop producing egg cases somewhere between 100 and 1,000. Nobody has narrowed that range
Why the gap matters A bed at 500 millionths holds healthy adult worms for months. They simply stop having young, the population ages out, and nothing visible goes wrong until it is too late
What this decides When stage 1 can stop. If a worm tolerates a little, the cleaning can finish early and the hydrogen bill falls. If it tolerates none, stage 1 has to run to the last of the perchlorate

Design

Six concentrations, three boxes each, ten worms per box, 18 boxes.

Box Perchlorate, millionths by weight
A none, the control
B 50
C 100
D 250
E 500
F 1,000
  • Everything else is held identical. Same compost, same moisture, same temperature, same darkness, same ten worms by weight.
  • Two things get counted. Survivors at day 14, which gives the death threshold. Egg cases at day 60, which gives the breeding threshold, the number we actually need.
  • Three boxes per level is not many. It is enough to say which way the answer falls, and the report will say plainly where the levels overlap.

The lab already has

Scale reading to 0.01 g
Somewhere lockable for a small bottle of oxidiser

On the perchlorate. Five grams is a small amount, and it is the one thing in this experiment that needs care. It is an oxidiser. Keep it away from anything that burns, and have it weighed by whoever normally weighs such things in your lab, not by a student.

Setup

On day one Why
1 Weigh the same mass of compost into each of the 18 boxes
2 Mix each concentration in distilled water first, then stir it through the compost. Controls get plain water, stirred the same way nothing goes in dry and uneven
3 Bring every box to the same dampness, the feel of a wrung-out cloth
4 Weigh ten worms into each box, by weight rather than count so a box of small worms does not quietly become a different experiment
5 Lids on, pierced. Dark. Room temperature. Note the date

What happens, and when

When What to do
Day 14 Tip each box out onto a tray, count living worms, return them. The only handling in the whole experiment
Every fortnight Check the dampness and add distilled water if any box has dried. Otherwise the boxes sit still
Day 60 Tip out and count the lemon-shaped egg cases, about the size of a match head. Count hatchlings too if there are any

What we expect to see

Day 14 Survival complete or near it in every box, since even the top level is below the published death threshold. Deaths at 1,000 would themselves be worth reporting
Day 60 The real result. Plenty of egg cases in the control, few or none at the top. Where the count collapses is the answer
What would surprise us Egg cases holding up at 250 or 500. That would let stage 1 stop early, and make the whole design cheaper in hydrogen

Recording rules

  • Count blind. Boxes are labelled by number and the key kept aside until the counting is finished.
  • Write down what is seen, not what it means. Any box that dries out, gets knocked over or is disturbed gets a note on the day, and the note stays in the report whatever it does to the result.

The sheets, blank and filled in

Spreadsheets, printed and filled in by hand at the bench, then typed up exactly as written. Every column is defined in the codebook. They are empty, because nobody has run this yet.

The sheet What goes in it
boxes.csv which box holds which dose, where it sits, and the weight of worms that went in
counts.csv live worms at day 14; egg cases and hatchlings at day 60
care_log.csv the fortnightly dampness check

The same sheets also exist with invented numbers in them, made by a script so the sheets can be checked before there is real data. This set is the expected outcome: breeding collapsing between 250 and 500 millionths. boxes · counts · care_log · a dry run, not a result

The analysis reads those sheets and writes a report: charts, a verdict for each question, and its own checks on the data. The rules that decide the verdicts were fixed before any data existed. See what that report looks like, built from the invented sheets, and it says dry run in its own title

Waste, and what happens to the worms

Control and low boxes compost and worms go to an ordinary garden bin
The top boxes not to a garden. Worms and compost are bagged and disposed of as your lab handles low-level chemical waste, because the perchlorate is still in there
Leftover perchlorate goes back to whoever holds it for you
