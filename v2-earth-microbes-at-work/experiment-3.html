Experiment 3: does an earthworm free phosphorus from Martian regolith?

Mars has ten times as much phosphorus as farm soil, all of it locked in a hard mineral called apatite. Step 2 of the design uses a bacterium to free it. But earthworms grind grit in a gizzard and carry a gut full of microbes, and nobody has ever measured whether an earthworm eating Martian regolith frees any phosphorus by itself. For a project called MarsWorm, that is the experiment to own.

What we would be asking of you
The question Does passing Martian regolith simulant through an earthworm make its phosphorus available to a plant?
Bench space 12 boxes on about half a metre of shelf
How long Ten weeks
Biosafety BSL-1. No genetic modification, no vertebrates
From the lab Shelf space, a scale to 0.01 g, a drying oven, and either a soil phosphorus test kit or twenty minutes on a spectrophotometer
From us Everything else, listed below
Student on site A session a week, short

Why nobody knows this

What is known Earthworms raise available phosphorus in ordinary compost. Their gut enzymes attack phosphorus held in dead plant matter
Why it does not carry over Martian regolith holds no dead plant matter. Its phosphorus is in rock, and that enzyme does nothing to rock
The open question Whether the rest of what a worm does, grinding grains in a gizzard and carrying acid-making microbes through its gut, gets at rock phosphorus. It might do nothing. It might do a little. Neither answer is written down anywhere
What it decides for the design Whether the rock bin in step 2 is load-bearing, or whether the earthworms in step 5 would do part of its job for free

Design

Four treatments, three boxes each, 12 boxes.

Box What is in it Why it is here
A simulant and compost, no worms the control
B simulant and compost, with worms the question: B against A
C simulant and compost, with worm castings but no live worm separates the live worm from what it leaves behind, as Experiment 1 does
D compost only, with worms the background: a rise that would have happened with no rock present

Every box starts with the same mass, the same dampness, and worms measured by weight rather than count.

  • The simulant is washed first. MGS-1 as sold carries no perchlorate, so this is about removing the fine salts that would otherwise sit in the results.

Setup

On day one Why
1 Mix simulant and compost, one part simulant to three parts compost the ratio the design uses, and the one worms tolerate
2 Weigh the same mass into each box. Box D gets compost alone
3 Damp everything to the same feel, a wrung-out cloth
4 Add worms by weight to B and D. Add castings to C
5 Take a day zero sample from every box and test it without this the experiment has nothing to compare against

What happens, and when

When What to do
Weeks 1 to 8 Leave them. Check dampness weekly, add distilled water as needed, note anything unusual
Week 8 Sample every box, dry the samples the same way for the same time, and test the phosphorus in one session so the method is identical across all twelve
Weeks 8 to 10 Sow radish in every box, thin to three seedlings, and at day 21 pull them, dry them and weigh them
  • The radish is the measure that counts. A test kit says what the chemistry sees. A radish says what a root can actually get. If the two disagree, believe the radish.

What we expect to see

Honestly, we do not know, which is why it is worth running.

If Then
B rises above A, and the radishes agree earthworms free rock phosphorus, and the design has a second route to it that costs nothing
B and A come out level a clean negative, just as useful. The worms are there for the organic matter and the rotting, and the whole phosphorus job stays with the bacterium in step 2
C rises as much as B the castings are doing the work and a live worm is not needed, which changes how a bed would be run

Recording rules

  • Every phosphorus sample runs in the same session, by the same method, on the same day. Split across two days and the comparison is gone.
  • Label boxes by number and keep the key aside, so whoever weighs the seedlings does not know which treatment they came from.
  • Note every watering, every disturbance, and every box that dries out. Those notes stay in the report whatever they do to the answer.

The sheets, blank and filled in

Four spreadsheets, printed and filled in by hand at the bench, then typed up exactly as written. Every column is defined in the codebook. They are empty, because nobody has run this yet.

The sheet What goes in it
boxes.csv which box holds which recipe, where it sits, and what went in by weight
phosphorus.csv the phosphorus test, on day zero and at week eight
radish.csv the seedlings from each box, dried and weighed. This is the answer
care_log.csv the weekly dampness check

The same four sheets also exist with invented numbers in them, made by a script so the sheets can be checked before there is real data. This set is the hoped-for outcome, where the worms free some of the phosphorus. boxes · phosphorus · radish · care log · a dry run, not a result

The analysis reads those sheets and writes a report: charts, a verdict for each question, and its own checks on the data. The rules that decide the verdicts were fixed before any data existed. See what that report looks like, built from the invented sheets, and it says dry run in its own title

Waste, and what happens to the worms

Everything here is compost, simulant and radish seedlings. It goes into an ordinary garden bin, and the worms go with it.
