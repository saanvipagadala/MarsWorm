Experiment 1: can earthworms turn Martian regolith into soil?

Step 5 of the design rests on one thing nobody has checked: that putting life into dead Martian regolith turns it into soil at all. Fifteen cups on a shelf check it.

What we would be asking of you
The question Do earthworms turn MGS-1 Martian regolith simulant into something a seedling will grow in? And does the earthworm have to be alive in the cup, or do its castings do the job on their own?
Bench space 15 cups on about half a metre of shelf
How long Seven weeks
Biosafety BSL-1. No genetic modification, no vertebrates
From the lab Shelf space, a scale, a drying oven, a pH meter or strips, and a light
From us Everything else, listed below
Student on site Short measuring sessions, a couple of days a week

Design

Five recipes, three identical cups of each, 15 cups. One recipe asks the question. The other four rule out a rival explanation.

Group What is in the cup Why it is here
1 Regolith only MGS-1 simulant, nothing added the floor: what Mars gives you
2 Regolith + organics MGS-1 + pre-composted leaves and scraps, no worms does the organic matter alone do it?
3 Regolith + organics + worms MGS-1 + organics + 3 Eisenia fetida the real thing: live earthworms doing the work
4 Regolith + organics + castings MGS-1 + organics + harvested castings, no live worms is the living worm needed at all?
5 Potting soil ordinary potting soil the ceiling: real soil, for scale
  • Groups 2 and 4 make this an experiment rather than a demonstration. Group 2 asks whether the food alone does the work. Group 4 asks whether the living worm is needed.
  • The castings for group 4 are made beforehand, in a small bin of soil and scraps run for two weeks, then harvested with the worms picked out.
  • Cup size is set by the worms. 8 oz cups, about 200 mL, with drainage holes. Smaller and a red wiggler cannot burrow, and the cup dries out in a day under a grow light.
  • Order 4 kg of simulant. The cups need about 3.1 kg. The spare kilogram is for rebuilding if the worms die in week 1 or a batch is spoiled.

Setup, on one afternoon

On day one Why
1 Build the 15 cups to the recipes above
2 Bury one cotton square in each cup dug up in weeks 2, 3 and 4; how much has rotted is the measure of how much life is in that cup
3 Lay the cups out in the shuffled bench_position order from containers.csv, never grouped by recipe grouped by recipe, one group quietly gets the brightest, warmest end of the shelf
4 Keep everything else identical same room, same 12 to 14 hours of light, same watering, for all fifteen cups

Phase 1, weeks 1 to 4: watching the regolith change

Nothing is planted yet. The worms work, and the regolith is measured around them. Six readings, once a week, from every cup, into data/weekly_measurements.csv.

The measurement How
1 Crumbs dry a 10 g scoop, tip onto the 2 mm sieve, shake, weigh what stays on top
2 Water held weigh that scoop dry, water until it stops dripping, weigh again
3 pH strip, in soil stirred into water
4 Nitrate aquarium strip, same cup
5 Cloth decay dig up one buried square in weeks 2, 3 and 4, then rinse, dry, weigh
6 Photo from directly above, same distance and lighting, coded by number
  • Return each scoop to the cup it came from. The cups are small, and four weeks of sampling would otherwise empty them.
  • Nothing is averaged or converted at the bench. Every line is one reading or one weighing.

Phase 2, weeks 4 to 7: finding out whether anything grows

Four weeks of measuring says whether the regolith looks more like soil. Planting says whether it is.

When What to do
1 Week 4 Sow 10 seeds of one species in every cup, all from the same packet
2 Every day Water identically, and log every watering
3 Every 2 to 3 days Count the sprouts that are up, and measure their heights with a ruler
4 Day 21 Cut the sprouts at soil level, dry them, and weigh them. This is the headline number
  • One seed packet on purpose. Seed batches germinate differently, and two packets would let the seeds win the experiment instead of the regolith.

What we expect to see

If life is what turns regolith into soil, the cups with live worms should grow the heaviest seedlings of any cup holding simulant, and the cups with the worms’ food or castings but no live worm should fall short. Written down before any weighing, so no rule can be picked afterwards.

Group What is in the cup What we expect
1 Martian simulant, nothing else almost nothing grows: this is the floor
2 Simulant + dead leaves a little growth, from the leaves alone
3 Simulant + leaves + live worms the most of any simulant cup
4 Simulant + leaves + worm castings more than leaves alone, less than live worms
5 Ordinary potting soil the most of all: real Earth soil, for scale

Recording rules

The rule What goes wrong without it
Write down only what an instrument reads a sheet saying “held 34% water” can never be checked, because the weights behind it are gone
A blank cell means not measured. Never type 0 a 0 is a measurement, and it says nothing grew. That is a different claim
All 15 cups measured on the same day a cup measured three days later has had three more days to change
Report all three cups of a group, never just the average an average hides the one cup that disagrees

The sheets, blank and filled in

Four spreadsheets, printed and filled in by hand at the bench, then typed up exactly as written. Every column is defined in the codebook. They are empty, because nobody has run this yet.

The sheet What goes in it
containers.csv which cup is which recipe, and where it sits on the shelf
weekly_measurements.csv the regolith, once a week for four weeks
plant_measurements.csv sprout counts and heights, every 2 to 3 days
harvest.csv day 21, the dried seedlings, weighed

The same four sheets also exist with invented numbers in them, made by a script to test the analysis before there is real data. This set is the hoped-for outcome. cups · weekly · plants · harvest · a dry run, not a result

The analysis reads those sheets and writes a report: charts, a verdict for each question, and its own checks on the data. The rules that decide the verdicts were fixed before any data existed. See what that report looks like, built from the invented sheets, and it says dry run in its own title

Waste, and what happens to the worms

Compost and potting soil ordinary waste
The worms rehomed to a garden or a compost bin. Eisenia fetida is a commercially sold composting organism, and is not released into wild habitat
Approvals none needed. No vertebrates, so no IACUC involvement
