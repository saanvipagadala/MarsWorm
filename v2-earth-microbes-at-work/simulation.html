Simulating the bioengineered organism on a Mars diet

Before asking anyone for a bench, we ran version 1 through a computer model of its own chemistry, fed only what Mars has. It did not hold. Here is what we used, what we did, what we found, and what it changed.

Overview · Three tracks · End to end

Inspiration

In 2026 scientists finished mapping a male fruit fly’s brain: every neuron and every connection between them, written down. That map is good enough that a computer copy of the brain can play a video game, with no fly involved.

A brain is a wiring diagram. A bacterium is a chemistry diagram: every reaction its cells can run. If a whole brain can be copied onto a computer and made to act, then the chemistry of one bacterium can be copied onto a computer and fed a Mars diet, before anyone orders a single gene. That is what this page does.

Why we did it

Version 1 ended with a design on paper: 37 genes, borrowed from 18 organisms, to go into one soil bacterium. Ordering those genes costs money and building the organism takes years. So we asked a cheaper question first. Does the chemistry even add up?

A living cell is a chemical factory. Scientists have written down, for a few well-studied bacteria, every reaction that factory can run. That list is called a metabolic model. Give a model a menu of food and it tells you whether the cell can grow, and how fast. It is not a living thing. It is a balance sheet.

The whole simulation runs on an ordinary laptop, with free software called cobrapy.

What we used

We used three published models. The first two come from the same lab at UC San Diego, which matters, because two models built the same way can be compared fairly. The third came from an expert on nitrogen fixation who read the design and kindly pointed us to a better organism.

Organism Why it is here Model Size
Azotobacter vinelandii The host version 1 chose. A soil bacterium that pulls nitrogen out of the air iAA1300 2,289 reactions
Rhodopseudomonas palustris A candidate host. A purple bacterium that can also use light iDT1294 2,721 reactions
Rhodobacter sphaeroides Recommended by an expert on nitrogen fixation. A purple bacterium that fixes nitrogen and makes no oxygen iRsp1140 1,901 reactions

We added the MarsWorm genes to each model as new reactions. The perchlorate genes, which split the poison and make oxygen from it, were the ones that mattered most.

What we did

We put each model on a Mars diet. A published model comes fed on laboratory food: sugar, ammonium and oxygen. Mars has none of those. So we shut off every input and opened only what Mars has.

On the Mars menu Off the menu
Carbon dioxide and nitrogen, from the air Oxygen
Water Ammonium and nitrate
The salts the Phoenix lander measured in wet regolith Sugar
Trace metals from the rock Acetate and other lab foods
Phosphate and iron, but only a little, because Mars locks them in minerals

Then we asked each model three questions.

The question Why it matters
Can it live on Mars food? If not, no amount of gene design helps
Can it live with the MarsWorm genes added? This is the version 1 design, tested
Does it make energy out of nothing? A real cell cannot. A broken model often does, and its numbers are then worthless

The answer each time is a growth rate: how fast the cell can make new cell material per hour. Zero means it cannot live.

What we found

The three hosts

Host On its lab menu On the Mars menu Verdict
Azotobacter vinelandii, the version 1 host grows ✗ cannot live, dark or lit, with or without the genes needs sugar and oxygen together. Mars has neither
Rhodopseudomonas palustris, the candidate grows ✗ cannot live in the light it needs a carbon source, which must come from the habitat
Rhodobacter, the expert’s recommendation grows ✓ lives, but only when handed hydrogen and light fixed nitrogen out of Mars air with no oxygen anywhere. That is the lit tank in version 2

Every growth number we had before this was computed on the lab menu. The Mars menu turned “it grows” into “it dies”.

What was wrong in the version 1 design

The fault What it means
A gene cassette was missing a gene. Splitting perchlorate leaves chloride behind. In the version 1 host, chloride had a way in and no way out, so it piled up and the pathway locked solid One added chloride pump unlocks it. That pump is not on the order list
The gene chosen to free phosphorus cannot work on Mars. It digests a store of phosphorus that only plants make, and Mars has never had a plant Mars phosphorus is locked in minerals, and only acid frees it. That is a different gene, or a different organism

What it changed

  • Each gene’s job was already being done by some microbe on Earth. A bacterium that destroys perchlorate exists. A bacterium that frees phosphorus from rock exists. A bacterium that turns air, hydrogen and light into food exists.
  • So use the microbe instead of moving the gene. Gene by gene, the list shrank, and it ended at none. That is the design version 2 can build now: five steps, three ordinary Earth microbes.
  • It is a real result, and not the whole answer. The question this project asks is whether you can design an organism to do this. Borrowed bacteria and plumbing answer a different question.
  • So version 2 holds both. It builds what can be built now, and keeps working toward the organism, as one of three tracks. The simulation did not kill the idea. It showed which parts of it were real.
