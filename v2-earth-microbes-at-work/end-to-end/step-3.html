Step 3. Turn Mars air into microbe and plant food

A purple bacterium grows on Mars air, hydrogen and light. The food it makes feeds the bacterium that destroys the perchlorate in step 4, and the soil bed in step 5.

End to end · ← Step 2 · Step 4 →

At a glance

In Lamps. Hydrogen. Mars air, which is mostly carbon dioxide with some nitrogen. Clean water from step 4, carrying the minerals that washed out of the regolith
Who does the work Rhodobacter, a purple bacterium. Nothing is fed to it. Like a plant, it grows on light and air, except that it uses hydrogen where a plant uses water
What happens Rhodobacter takes carbon and nitrogen out of the Mars air and grows. As it grows it makes two things that are useful to us
Food 1: sour acids Rhodobacter turns more carbon into sugars and acids than it can use. The extra leaks out of the cells into the water as sour acids, small carbon molecules a bit like the sharpness in lemon juice. Those acids are the food that Azospira, the bacterium that destroys the perchlorate in step 4, lives on
Food 2: the cells themselves The nitrogen Rhodobacter took from the air is now protein inside its cells. There are more cells than went in. Those cells become the organic matter and, once they die, the plant food in the growing bed
Out The water with the sour acids in it, piped to the dark tank in step 4 as food for Azospira
The Rhodobacter cells, to the growing bed in step 5

Why hydrogen

  • Mars air is carbon dioxide, and carbon dioxide is carbon that has already been burnt. You cannot build anything living out of ash. To turn it back into food you have to un-burn it, and un-burning means putting hydrogen back on. The hydrogen is not fuel here. It is the raw material.
  • Light does the other half. It gives Rhodobacter the energy to do that un-burning. Light supplies energy only, and no hydrogen of its own.
  • Plants get their hydrogen by splitting water, and breathe out the oxygen. This bacterium cannot split water, so we hand it the hydrogen instead.

Where the hydrogen comes from

  • From splitting water with electricity, and Mars has a great deal of water as buried ice.
  • Every serious Mars plan already does this, for rocket fuel and for breathing air. The machine is already there. We ask it for a slice.
  • A small slice. Cleaning a tonne of regolith needs about a kilogram of hydrogen, which a propellant plant makes in minutes. Splitting that water also releases oxygen the crew breathes, and the hydrogen turns back into water in the dark tank. What is really spent is a little electricity.

Why this tank must have no oxygen in it

  • Rhodobacter pulls nitrogen out of the air with an enzyme called nitrogenase, and oxygen destroys that enzyme. Any microbe that makes oxygen and fixes nitrogen at the same time is fighting itself.
  • Rhodobacter makes no oxygen. A plant makes oxygen because it splits water. Rhodobacter cannot split water, so it never makes any. That is why its nitrogenase keeps working, and it is the main reason we chose it.
  • Rhodobacter also shuts down if oxygen appears. It senses oxygen and stops using the light. On Earth that protects it. In our tank it would stop the food supply.
  • Destroying perchlorate gives off oxygen. So the perchlorate cannot be dealt with in this tank. That job goes to a second tank in step 4, kept dark and kept separate, with a different bacterium in it.

How the acids and the bacteria are separated

The lit tank ends up holding one murky liquid with both products in it: the sour acids dissolved in the water, and the Rhodobacter cells floating in it. They are wanted in two different places, so they have to be parted.

  • The lit tank itself is stirred all the time. Any Rhodobacter that sinks to the bottom is sitting in the dark. In the dark it stops making acids and starts eating the acids around it. So the working tank never stands still.
  • A second, smaller vessel with no lamp stands beside the lit tank. A slow trickle of the murky liquid is drawn from the lit tank into this one, and this one is left still.
  • In the still vessel the bacteria sink, the way mud settles in a jar of pond water. The clear liquid on top is the water with the acids in it. The sludge on the bottom is the bacteria.
  • Draw off the top and pipe it to the dark tank in step 4. Scrape off the bottom and take it to the growing bed in step 5. No power, no moving parts.

Why it matters

  • This is the only step that puts something new into the regolith. Step 1 and step 4 take the perchlorate away. Step 2 unlocks phosphorus and iron that were already in the rock. Step 3 is the one that adds what Mars does not have: nitrogen, which becomes plant food, and living cells, which become the organic matter.
  • The nitrogen does not come out as a fertiliser you could pour on. It is locked inside the Rhodobacter cells as protein. It stays there until the cells are mixed into the bed and die, and only then can a plant root take it up. That is what the earthworms in step 5 are for.
  • Why not skip the tank and mix Rhodobacter straight into the regolith? Because regolith is dark, and in the dark Rhodobacter cannot use light to make food. In the tank it is a factory. In the regolith it would be one more mouth eating what the factory made.
