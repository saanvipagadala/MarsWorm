Step 2. Free the phosphorus and iron

A bacterium that gives off a weak acid dissolves the rock a little, and frees the phosphorus and iron locked inside it.

End to end · ← Step 1 · Step 3 →

At a glance

In The washed regolith from step 1, still damp
Who does the work Bacillus megaterium, an ordinary soil bacterium. Gardeners buy it by the bag to free phosphorus in flower beds
What happens The bacterium sits on the bits of crushed rock and gives off a weak acid. The acid slowly dissolves the rock around it, and the phosphorus and iron that were locked inside the rock come loose, in a form a plant root can take up
How long Three weeks, in a bin. Damp, not flooded. Closer to a compost heap than a tank
Out Regolith with reachable phosphorus and iron, with the bacteria still living in it, to the growing bed in step 5

Why it is needed

  • Mars has plenty of phosphorus and none of it is reachable. Ten times as much as Earth farm soil, locked inside a hard mineral called apatite. Think of sugar sealed in glass.
  • Mars is red because its iron is rust. Roots cannot take up rust. The same acid that frees the phosphorus also dissolves a little of the rust, and that turns the iron into a form a root can use.
  • Without this step the bed holds minerals a plant can see and cannot touch.

Why it works this way

  • Damp is enough. Bacteria cannot work dry and acid only works in water, but a thin film of moisture around each bit of rock is all it takes. Nothing has to be drained or separated afterwards.
  • The bacteria are not filtered out. They carry on doing the same job in the bed, and when they die they become part of the organic matter the earthworms feed on.
  • It has been done on Moon dust. In three weeks it freed far more phosphorus than a crop needs, and tobacco grown in the treated dust came out about three times bigger. Nobody has tried it on Mars regolith yet.
