Stage 5: Building MarsWorm

How do 37 genes become one living microbe?

Stage 2 ends with 37 genes and 41,445 letters of DNA. That is a parts list. Nobody has built anything yet.

This stage turns the list into something alive: 24 genes go into a real bacterium, in six rounds, and every round is tested before the next one is ordered. Then comes the test the whole project has been aiming at, which is Stage 6: put MarsWorm into Martian dirt, plant a seed, and find out whether anything grows.

The microbe that carries them

Genes have to live inside something. Stage 2 could not choose what, because it searched proteins, and whether a creature breathes air or can be worked on in a lab is a fact about creatures.

The shopping list answers it anyway. Every gene on it was copied from something alive, and that something still has it. Count who donated the most and one name is far ahead: Azotobacter vinelandii, a common soil bacterium, which donated 13 of the 37.

Being the biggest donor is not a good enough reason on its own. Three other things make it the right choice, and one of them is the boldest bet in the whole project: the nitrogen genes are destroyed by oxygen, the perchlorate genes make oxygen, and this microbe happens to be one of the hungriest oxygen sinks known. Nobody has ever tried putting those two together inside it.

The DNA comes in the post

Nobody catches an Antarctic fish or digs up a Deinococcus to cut a gene out of it. DNA is printed to order. You send a company the letters, they build the molecule, and they post it to you.

The company Builds in one piece
Twist Bioscience 300 to 5,000 letters
IDT gBlocks 125 to 3,000 letters
GenScript GenWand up to 10,000 letters, double-stranded

All 37 sequences were run past those published rules, and every one of them can be made.

What comes back is a chemical, not a creature: a dried speck you cannot see, in a small plastic tube, posted at room temperature. Nothing in it is alive. The tube looks empty because there is almost nothing in it to look at.

What actually arrives, and what the lab does with it

The order: six cassettes, not 37 genes

Only 24 genes get ordered, because Azotobacter already has the other 13. They travel in six cassettes, each carrying a group of genes plus the switches and markers that make them work.

2 Damage: radiation, chemicals and repair 6 8,608 $600–1,290 Shine UV on it, splash it with bleach-like chemicals. Does it live?
3 The Mars poison, and breathing without air 5 10,110 $710–1,520 Is the poison gone, and is it still making fertiliser?
4 Making food, and building the home 3 6,055 $420–910 Does it grow with no food added, and build itself a slimy home?
5 Breaking down what is dead 4 8,153 $570–1,220 Put it on plant fibre, shells, starch and fat. Do they disappear?
6 Unlocking the rock 2 6,492 $450–970 Does iron and phosphorus come out of ground-up rock?
Total 24 45,666 $3,200–6,900

Cassette 3 is the one to watch. It is the biggest, the dearest, and the only one whose answer is genuinely unknown, because it is the oxygen bet. It is also 110 letters over GenScript’s limit, so it needs a small trim or a second tube.

At 7 to 15 cents a letter the whole order comes to $3,200 to $6,900. That is an order of magnitude rather than a quote, since long pieces are priced case by case.

But you never buy all six at once. Each cassette is ordered only after the one before it has passed its test, so the first cheque is about $440. Those six results are written down as they happen, one row per round, in build_log.csv. It lives in Stage 6 because that is what reads it: the growth trial will not report a single number until all six rounds say yes. A half-built microbe planted in dirt is not a weaker experiment. It is a different one.

Getting the DNA inside

None of this needs CRISPR. Azotobacter takes up loose DNA when it is hungry, and slots it into its own chromosome. Both of those are things it does by itself, which is one more reason it was chosen.

What this stage still gets wrong

Nobody has built this, and the hardest round is round 3. The oxygen pairing is a real prediction, not a demonstration, which is exactly why it is tested third of six, before anything expensive depends on it.

It needs a lab. Everything before this stage runs on a laptop or a kitchen table. This one needs a microbiology bench, someone qualified to work at it, and an institution willing to place the order.

Two other designs were worked out and turned down. Their reasons are kept, because a design with no rejected alternatives was never really a choice.
