What arrives in the box, and what the lab does with it

Stage 5 ends with an order placed: six cassettes, 45,666 letters of DNA, printed by a company and posted to a lab.

This is what happens next. A box turns up. Somebody opens it. And a few days later there is a living thing in it that was not alive before.

The box

A few weeks after the order goes in, a padded envelope arrives. What is inside is smaller and duller than anybody expects.

What’s inside A dried white speck. Usually you cannot see it at all
In A small plastic tube, smaller than your little finger
How much Fifty billionths of a gram
Posted Ordinary room temperature. Dried DNA does not spoil

The tube looks empty. That is normal, and it is worth knowing before you open it and think the company forgot to put anything in.

The first thing the lab does is add a drop of liquid and let the speck dissolve. Ten minutes. The DNA is now ready to use.

What’s on the DNA

Here is the surprise: our genes are the smallest part of it.

A gene is only an instruction for building a protein. It does not say when to build it, where to sit in the chromosome, or how anyone would know. All of that has to be written onto the same piece of DNA, or the cell just ignores it.

So each cassette is our genes wrapped in machinery. In order, front to back:

The part What it does
Landing arms, one at each end Copied from the microbe’s own DNA. This is what makes the cassette stay
The switch (promoter) Tells the cell to read the genes. Nothing happens without it. We borrow nifH’s, see below
A start mark before each gene Tells the cell where each instruction begins
Our genes The actual point of the whole thing
A glow gene Makes the cells green when the switch is on. our checkpoint
A stop mark Tells the cell to stop reading here
A survival gene lets the right cells live through the test plate

The machinery costs about 3,000 letters on every cassette, which is why a cassette carrying 3,000 letters of genes is a 6,000-letter order.

Only the genes are ours. The arms are copied from the published Azotobacter vinelandii genome. The glow gene and the survival gene are catalogue parts, used unchanged in thousands of labs.

And the switch we borrow from the microbe itself. A switch that works beautifully in one bacterium can be completely silent in another, so we take one we know works here: the switch in front of nifH, the gene Azotobacter uses to run its own nitrogen machine. It is this organism’s own switch, doing the job it already does, in front of our genes instead.

The company prints exactly the letters we send, so every one of these parts is in the order. Nothing is added later, and nothing arrives by itself.

Why one long piece, and not a circle

DNA can be ordered as a circle (a plasmid) that floats around inside the cell, or as one long straight piece. We want the straight piece, because we want the genes to end up in the microbe’s own chromosome, not floating beside it.

That matters for a simple reason: things in the chromosome get copied every time the cell divides. The microbe becomes the design. Freeze a spoonful in the freezer and you have a permanent, free supply for ever.

Straight DNA also goes into Azotobacter two to three times better than a circle does. (cited)

Getting it inside

A microbe is not waiting for post. It has a wall, and loose DNA outside it is just litter.

But Azotobacter will swallow DNA by itself, when it is hungry in a very particular way. Grow it in food that is short of iron and short of nitrogen, and it starts pulling in whatever DNA is floating nearby, hoping something useful is in it. This is called natural competence, and it is one of the main reasons this microbe was chosen.

So the lab does not force anything open. It makes the cells hungry for two days, then tips our tube in.

Day What happens
0 Dissolve the speck. Ten minutes
1–2 Grow the cells in hungry food: low iron, no nitrogen
3 Add the DNA. The cells take it in and the landing arms slot it into the chromosome
4 The test plate
5 Check it really went in, and freeze a spoonful as the permanent copy
6 → The experiment. Freeze it, thaw it, count who lived, modified against plain

The lab does days 0 to 5. The science is everything after.

One nice detail: because the arms match the microbe’s own DNA, they make the whole thing work 10 to 50 times better than DNA with no arms. (cited)

How you know it worked

Out of millions of cells, only a tiny handful actually take the DNA in. And you cannot tell them apart by looking. They are the same size, same shape, same colour.

So the cassette carries two extra passengers whose only job is to answer that.

The survival gene, and a poisoned plate. Spread everything onto a dish laced with an antibiotic. Ordinary Azotobacter dies on contact. A cell that took our cassette has the survival gene and lives. The plate is not somewhere the microbe happens to survive. It is a filter that kills everything except what you are looking for.

The glow gene, and a checkpoint. The colonies that grow should also be green, because the glow gene shares a switch with our genes. Green means the switch is on and the proteins are being made. Colonies that grow but stay dark mean stop. The switch does not work in this microbe, and no experiment after this point can succeed. Better to find that out on day 4 than three months later.

Isn’t an antibiotic-resistant microbe a scary thing to make? It is a fair question and there is a plain answer. It is a laboratory tag, and it is how essentially all genetic engineering is done, including the kits sold to schools. Azotobacter vinelandii is BSL-1: harmless, a common soil bacterium, not something that lives in people. Everything is sterilised before it is thrown away. And the tag is taken back out again after each round, so the finished MarsWorm carries no antibiotic resistance at all; see the other five rounds.

The glowing-bacteria kit

Lots of school science classes do a version of this: the Bio-Rad pGLO experiment, where you chill a plate, warm it, and jellyfish DNA goes into E. coli and the bacteria glow green.

Can we just do that? Half of it, yes. Here is the honest comparison.

pGLO at school Ours
What arrives Dried DNA in a tube ✅ The same
Dissolve it in liquid Yes ✅ The same
Carries a survival gene Yes ✅ The same
Poisoned plate to find the winners Yes ✅ The same
It glows Yes ✅ The same
The microbe E. coli ❌ Azotobacter
How the cell opens Cold, then 42 °C for 50 seconds ❌ Two days of being hungry
Where the DNA ends up Floating loose ❌ Built into the chromosome, for ever
What the glow means The glow is the answer ❌ A checkpoint. The answer is counting survivors after the freezer
How long One class period ❌ About a week

So why can’t we chill it and warm it? Because the heat trick is a fact about E. coli, not a fact about DNA. Azotobacter opens for a different reason entirely. Hunger, not shock. And the 42 °C step is not merely useless here: uptake in Azotobacter is actually damaged by 42 °C, so the school protocol would work against us. (cited)

Everything that kit teaches still transfers. DNA in a tube. Cells that swallow it. A plate that kills everything else. A glow that says it worked. Only the opening the cell step changes, and it changes because we are not using E. coli.

There is one real difference in what you buy. pGLO is a finished product, somebody designed that plasmid years ago, switch and all, and the kit ships the same one to every school. Ours is printed to order, so every part on the DNA is a part we chose and wrote into the file. That is the price of a design nobody has built before.

Is this CRISPR?

No. None of it. And that is a result worth saying out loud, not a gap.

What we are doing is older and has its own names:

The step What it is called
The cell swallowing DNA from around it Natural transformation
The arms slotting the cassette into the chromosome Homologous recombination
The two together Gene targeting
Snipping the antibiotic tag back out afterwards Site-specific recombination

This is classical bacterial genetics, and it predates CRISPR by decades. It works here for exactly one reason: Azotobacter already does both halves by itself. It swallows DNA when starved, and it swaps matching DNA into its own chromosome. We are not adding a tool. We are using two things the microbe was already doing, and the landing arms are just how we aim it.

CRISPR is the right answer for a host that can’t do this. That is most hosts. It isn’t this one.

CRISPR does turn up in one place: the delivery plans price a version using CAST, a CRISPR tool that inserts big pieces of DNA rather than cutting them. It is plan 3 of 5, kept as a backup. It has never been demonstrated in Azotobacter, which is exactly why it is not plan 1.

Start with one

We do not order all six cassettes at once. We order cassette 1, and nothing else, and we find out whether it worked before spending another penny.

The gene What it does From
cspB Keeps the cell working in the cold A soil bacterium
Antifreeze Maxi Stops ice tearing the cell apart Winter flounder
CAS31 Protects the cell as it dries out A pea-family plant
TPS1 Makes a sugar that stands in for missing water A yeast

Four genes, 6,248 letters, about $440–940, the cheapest cassette in the build but one, and the easiest to test. Freeze it and thaw it. Dry it out. Did more survive than the plain microbe did?

It is also carrying a second question, and that one matters more. All six cassettes use the same switch, the same arms, the same method, so cassette 1 is really a test of the whole recipe. It answers that on day 4, from the colour of the plate, long before any freezing experiment starts. If the colonies grow but stay dark, the recipe is wrong for every cassette after it too, and we found out for $440 instead of $6,900.

That is the whole reason for going one cassette at a time: every round is a real step towards MarsWorm, and every round is also a chance to stop.

The other five rounds

Same box, same hungry cells, same plate, five more times. Every round starts from the frozen spoonful the round before produced, so the microbe keeps everything it already learned and adds one more group of abilities.

The microbe can now…
Round 1 survive freezing and drying, and the switch is proven
Round 2 + survive radiation and harsh chemicals
Round 3 + clear the Mars poison, and live without air
Round 4 + make its own food, and build itself a home
Round 5 + break down anything dead
Round 6 + pull food out of solid rock

Almost everything stays identical each time: the hungry food, the plate, the checks. Three things have to change:

  • A different landing spot in the chromosome, or the new cassette would overwrite the last one
  • A different antibiotic tag, because the microbe is already immune to the last one
  • A different glow colour, so you can look at a plate and see which rounds worked, not just that one did

The problem this runs into

You run out of antibiotics. There are only a handful that work, and six rounds would need six. By round four the microbe is immune to everything and nothing can select round five.

The fix is standard and rather neat: put a pair of markers either side of the antibiotic tag, and afterwards add an enzyme that snips it back out. The ability stays; the tag leaves. The same antibiotic is then free to use again.

Which gives two things worth saying:

  1. Six rounds need only two or three antibiotics
  2. The finished MarsWorm carries no antibiotic resistance at all. The tags were scaffolding, and scaffolding comes down

Where it stops being routine

Rounds 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 are the same job with different cargo. Round 3 is not.

Round 3 is the cassette that makes oxygen inside a microbe whose most valuable machine is destroyed by oxygen. It is the one round that is a guess rather than a procedure, and its test asks three things at once: is the poison gone, is it still making fertiliser, and can it grow without air? The middle one is the whole bet.

Everything before round 3 is deliberately cheap, so that finding this out costs as little as it possibly can.

What could go wrong

Honestly, and in rough order of likelihood.

What could go wrong What it would mean
Nobody agrees to do the work The most likely failure of all, and it has nothing to do with biology
The plate grows but nothing glows The switch is not turning our genes on the way it turns on nifH. Caught on day 4, before anything expensive
The hungry cells refuse to swallow Common. The backup is a machine that zaps the DNA in with electricity, one of five routes the lab can choose from
The fish protein doesn’t fold Antifreeze Maxi comes from a winter flounder. Nothing guarantees a bacterium can build it properly. This failure looks like “no difference after freezing”
Round 3’s bet is wrong The oxygen from one gene wrecks the nitrogen machine of another. This is the real scientific risk in the whole design
A later round won’t select The microbe is already immune to that antibiotic. This is why tags get snipped out
An earlier ability goes quiet Piling on cassettes can switch off older ones. The per-round colours are how you would notice
It grows too slowly to be useful 24 extra genes is a lot of work for one cell. Not measurable until it happens
It all works and nothing changes A real result, and Stage 4’s design already tells this apart from a failed experiment
