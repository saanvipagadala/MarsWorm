Stage 1: Searching Earth for MarsWorm

Is there already something on Earth that could do this?

That question has to come first. Making soil on Mars needs one organism that does two unrelated things at once: survive Mars, and enrich the dirt, turning dust into something a plant can live in. If such an organism already exists, there is nothing to design. Find it, and use it.

So this stage scores 322 sequenced organisms against 23 specific abilities, and plots them on one map.

The answer is no, and it is not close. Nothing gets past 8 of 13 on survival or 7 of 10 on enrichment. The best total is Arabidopsis thaliana at 14 of 23, and that is a plant, which would freeze solid on Mars before doing any of it. The corner of the map where an organism does the whole job is empty.

That empty corner is why Stage 2 exists.

Everything after this rests on the gap being real. If some organism already both survived Mars and enriched the dirt, the other five stages would be wasted effort.

How an organism is scored

The job splits into 23 specific jobs, 13 for survival and 10 for enrichment. Every organism is asked the same question, once per job:

Does this organism have a protein that can fill this job at all?

Take Deinococcus radiodurans. Ask it mend the DNA that gets broken anyway and the answer is yes, so it takes the point. Ask it chew up tough plant stalks and the answer is no. Twenty-three questions later it has a score: 6 out of 13 on survival, 0 out of 10 on enrichment.

One point per job, however well an organism does it. Forty starch enzymes are worth no more than one, because the job is either covered or it is not. That cap is what stops the best-studied organisms winning on paperwork.

What this score cannot see

The earthworm scores zero on survival and zero on enrichment. The animal that actually does this job on Earth (Lumbricus terrestris) sits in the bottom-left corner of a map about doing this job, because only 27 earthworm proteins have ever been reviewed, and they are blood pigment, muscle and the energy chain. Not one is a soil enzyme. That zero is a fact about the database, not about the worm, and the map marks it with a second, hollow dot showing where the earthworm would probably sit if anyone had done the work.

So an empty score can mean an empty shelf. That does not weaken the finding, because missing data runs the wrong way to help: the organisms at the top of this map are the best-studied on Earth, with every advantage that gives. If they cannot survive Mars and enrich the dirt, nothing on Earth can.

The second limit, plainly: having a gene is not the same as thriving. This measures what an organism has a protein for, not whether it would survive Mars.
