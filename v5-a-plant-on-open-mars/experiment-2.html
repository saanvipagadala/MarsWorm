Experiment 2: can a plant dry out and revive, night after night?

Version 5 has the plant dry out completely every Martian night, because antifreeze proteins buy a few degrees and a Martian night needs eighty. Real resurrection plants do dry out and revive. They do it a handful of times a year. Version 5 would ask for several hundred. Nobody has measured what happens then, and this experiment does.

What we would be asking of you
The question How many dry and wet cycles can a resurrection plant or a desert moss take before it stops coming back?
Bench space A shelf, a light, and a timer
How long A year, unattended most of the time
Biosafety BSL-1. Plants and water. Nothing else
From the lab Shelf space, a light on a timer, a scale, and ideally a chlorophyll fluorometer
From us The plants, the protocol, and the analysis
Student on site Ten minutes a week

Why this is the cheapest experiment in the project

It is trays of moss and a timer. There is no instrument that has to be bought, no chemical that has to be locked up, and no organism that needs containment. What it costs is patience.

It is also the most likely single thing to end version 5, which makes it very good value.

What we are looking at What it says
What is known Resurrection plants and desert mosses lose nearly all their water, stop, and restart when water returns. One desert moss has been through simulated Martian conditions and recovered
Why the gap matters Every published case we can find is occasional. A few times a year, or years apart. Drying out is not free, and surviving it once says nothing about surviving it a thousand times
What this decides Whether job 2 is possible at all. If a plant wears out after thirty cycles, version 5 does not work, however good its genes are

Design

Four cycling rates, six trays each, 24 trays.

How often it dries and rewets
A daily, which is what Mars would demand
B weekly
C monthly
D never. Kept damp throughout
  • Tray D is the control that matters, because plants get old and die on their own, and without it a decline in tray A proves nothing.
  • The rates are a ladder, not a pair. If damage adds up per cycle then A fails first and C much later. If it adds up with time instead, all four fail together, and the drying is not what is doing it.
  • Two species, a desert moss and a resurrection plant, because they may not behave the same way at all.

Materials

What How much
We bring
Syntrichia caninervis or a similar desert moss enough for 12 trays
A resurrection plant such as Selaginella lepidophylla enough for 12 trays
Trays, labels, and a watering schedule 24
The lab already has
Shelf space with a light on a timer about 1 m
A scale reading to 0.01 g for the wet and dry weights
A chlorophyll fluorometer the measurement that matters. If you do not have one, greenness from photographs is a usable substitute

On the fluorometer. It measures whether photosynthesis restarts, which is the real question. A plant can look green and be dead. If one is not available, photograph every tray under fixed lighting and measure colour, and say in the report that it is a proxy.

Setup

Why
1 Establish all 24 trays and let them settle for a month before any cycling so the starting point is a healthy plant, not a shipped one
2 Record wet weight, dry weight and a fluorometer reading for every tray these are the numbers everything is compared against
3 Begin cycling. Dry to constant weight, then rewet to the starting weight drying to a weight rather than a time keeps every cycle the same
4 Randomise the trays on the shelf and keep the key aside

What happens, and when

When What to do
Every cycle Note the dry weight reached, and the time taken to rewet
Every month A fluorometer reading on every tray, two hours after rewetting. This is the recovery number
When a tray stops recovering Record the cycle count and leave it in place. Some recover later
At a year Final readings and a dry weight for everything

What we expect to see

Where to look What we expect
Tray D Slow decline at most. This is what ageing alone looks like
Trays A, B and C Recovery falling with the number of cycles rather than with time
The number that matters How many cycles a plant manages before recovery drops by half. That number decides version 5
What would end it Tray A failing within a few dozen cycles. A Martian year is about 670 days, so anything under a few hundred means the plant cannot last a season
What would surprise us All four trays declining together. That would mean time, not drying, is what wears the plant out, and nightly drying costs nothing

Recording rules

  • Label by number and keep the key aside until the year is finished.
  • Write down what is seen, not what it means. A tray that dries unevenly, gets watered twice, or grows mould gets a note on the day, and the note stays in the report.
  • Do not throw a failed tray away. Some plants recover after looking dead for weeks, and a discarded tray cannot show that.

The sheets, blank and filled in

Spreadsheets, printed and filled in by hand at the bench, then typed up exactly as written. Every column is defined in the codebook. They are empty, because nobody has run this yet.

The sheet What goes in it
trays.csv which tray is on which cycling rate, which species is in it, and where it sits on the shelf
cycles.csv one row per drying. The dry weight reached, and how long it took to rewet
recovery.csv the monthly fluorometer reading, two hours after rewetting. The sheet the answer comes from
care_log.csv the weekly check, and anything that went wrong

The same sheets also exist with invented numbers in them, made by a script, so the sheets can be checked before there is real data. This set is the expected outcome: recovery falling with the number of dryings rather than with time, and the daily trays failing first. trays · cycles · recovery · care_log · a dry run, not a result
