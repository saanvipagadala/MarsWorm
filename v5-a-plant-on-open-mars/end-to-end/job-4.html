Job 4: reach the water

A plant that has solved everything else still has to drink. There is no rain on Mars, no rivers, and no damp ground. This page is about where the water is and what it takes to get at it.

Where the water is

There is a great deal of water on Mars. All of it is either frozen or locked into rock.

Where How far down
Mid latitudes, around 55 to 58 degrees ice starts 1 to 2 metres down, and can be over 100 metres thick
Higher latitudes, where the Phoenix lander dug a few centimetres

So water is not the scarce thing on Mars. It is simply underneath, and frozen.

Why frozen is not the same as unavailable

Ice by itself is no use to a root. But Martian ground is full of salt, and salt against ice makes liquid water without any warming at all. Salt lowers the melting point. That is the same reason salt clears an icy road.

So at the boundary where salty Martian ground meets buried ice, there is brine: liquid water, at temperatures far below freezing, continuously made as long as the ice is there.

That fits this design unusually well, because job 1 has already forced the whole plant to work below freezing. A plant that has to stay cold anyway is exactly the plant that can drink water which only exists when it is cold.

The catch, and it is severe

Brine is salty by definition, and the saltier it is, the colder it stays liquid and the harder it is to drink. Salty water clings to its own water and a root has to pull much harder to take any.

There is a measured floor to this. Nothing on Earth grows when water is held more tightly than a certain point, and Martian brines sit close to that line.

Kind of brine How available its water is Freezes at
Ordinary salt 0.75, comfortably usable -21 °C
Perchlorate, which Martian dirt is full of 0.5 to 0.6, at or below the floor for life -70 °C

The best antifreeze on Mars is also the least drinkable. So version 5’s plant wants ordinary salty brine, which means it wants ground where the perchlorate has been broken down into plain chloride.

And version 4’s plant is already built to do that. The genes it carries to survive the chlorine salts break them down and leave plain chloride. So the plant improves its own water supply while it cleans the ground it stands in. It is the only place in this design where two problems solve each other.

The catch is that only the full version of version 4 breaks perchlorate down. Its one certain gene deals with chlorate. Perchlorate needs two more genes, and version 4 marks those two as its weakest part.

What the plant needs

What Why
Deep roots One to two metres, to reach ice. There is a known gene that controls how steeply a root grows down
The ability to pull hard Roots drink by holding their own water more tightly than the soil does. Plants that live in salt build up substances inside their cells to win that contest
Salt handling Whatever it drinks comes with salt in it. Version 4’s plant already puts salt into storage sacs
Never to uncover the ice Exposed Martian ice evaporates away. A root reaching ice must not open a path for it to escape

What this does to where version 5 can live

It ties the design to a map. The plant only works where ice is close enough to the surface for a root to reach, and warm enough for the brine at its edge to be drinkable rather than perchlorate-strong.

Those two pull against each other. Shallow ice is at high latitudes, which are cold. Warmer ground is nearer the middle latitudes, where the ice is deeper. And job 1 wants the deepest basin on the planet for its thicker air. That is somewhere else again.

No site on Mars has all three. Choosing between them is the first real calculation version 5 would have to do, and it is a calculation nobody has run, including us.

Where this leaves job 4

On paper. The genes for deep roots and for pulling water out of salty ground all exist and all work. What does not exist is any measurement of a root reaching brine at the edge of buried ice, at Martian temperature, and getting anything out of it.

That would need a chamber, a column of frozen simulant and a lot of patience, and it is the most expensive experiment anybody in this project has proposed.
