Job 2: survive the night

A Martian night is colder than anywhere on Earth outside a laboratory freezer. This page is about why the obvious answer does not work, and what the plant has to do instead.

How cold it gets

Where and when How cold
Martian ground, on a summer afternoon at the right latitude up to about 20 °C
Martian ground, the same night down to about -80 °C
The coldest natural temperature ever recorded on Earth about -89 °C

So every single Martian night is close to the worst night Earth has ever had. And it happens again the next day, and the next, for the life of the plant.

Why ice is what does the damage

Cold on its own does not kill a cell. Ice does. Water expands as it freezes and the crystals tear cell walls apart from the inside. A plant that freezes solid and thaws is usually mush. That is what happens to lettuce in a freezer.

The obvious answer, and why we dropped it

The obvious answer is antifreeze. Some fish and insects make proteins that stop their body fluids freezing, and those proteins have been moved into plants before.

The numbers are nowhere near enough.

How much freezing point it buys
Fish antifreeze proteins about 1 °C
Insect antifreeze proteins over 5 °C
What a Martian night needs about 80 °C

It is not a shortfall that better engineering closes. It is the wrong order of magnitude by a factor of fifteen.

And it has already been tried. Somebody put a fish antifreeze protein into tobacco and measured what happened. The plant made the protein, and it was no more frost resistant than an ordinary one.

What antifreeze proteins are genuinely good at is something smaller and still useful: stopping small ice crystals joining into big damaging ones. That is worth having as a second line, and it is not a solution.

What actually works

Have no water left to freeze.

Some plants can lose almost all of the water in their cells, stop completely, and sit dried out for months or years. When water comes back they restart, sometimes within hours. They are called resurrection plants, and there are mosses that do the same thing.

A cell with almost no water in it cannot form damaging ice, because there is nothing left to freeze. Instead of fighting the cold, the plant removes what the cold could damage.

What that costs the design, and it is a lot

A dried-out plant is not doing anything. It is not photosynthesising, not growing, and not taking up water. It is stopped.

So version 5’s plant is only alive for part of the time, and this needs saying plainly rather than being buried:

The situation What it means
When it works Daylight hours when the ground is cold enough to hold water and bright enough to photosynthesise
When it is stopped Every night, every winter, and any day too warm for job 1
What that means for yield Very little. A plant that works for a few hours a day for part of the year grows at a small fraction of an Earth plant’s rate

Version 5 cannot honestly promise a plant that flourishes. It promises one that lives, works when it can, and waits the rest of the time. Anything more would be a claim the physics does not support.

The gap between drying out and doing it every day

There is one more problem and it is not solved anywhere.

Resurrection plants dry out occasionally. A desert moss might do it a few times a year, or go years between. Version 5 would ask a plant to do it every single night. That is several hundred times a year, for years.

Nobody has measured whether anything can take that. Drying out and reviving is not free, and doing it once is not evidence that you can do it a thousand times. This is the single biggest unknown in version 5 and it is why this job is marked as being on paper rather than designed.
