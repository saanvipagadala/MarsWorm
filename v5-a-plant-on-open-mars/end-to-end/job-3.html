Job 3: block the light that harms

Mars has no ozone layer, so the ultraviolet light that Earth’s atmosphere filters out reaches the Martian ground in full. This is the easiest of the four jobs, and it is worth saying why.

What reaches the ground

Earth has an ozone layer that absorbs the most damaging part of sunlight before it arrives. Mars does not. The harshest ultraviolet light lands on the Martian surface unfiltered, and it is strong enough to sterilise exposed cells.

That sounds like one of the great obstacles to life on Mars, and it is usually listed as one. It is the cheapest problem on the list to solve, and this project has now run into that fact twice.

Why it is cheap

Ultraviolet light is stopped by almost anything. A fraction of a millimetre of dust blocks it completely. So does a layer of pigment. In the driest deserts on Earth, microbes live a millimetre or two down inside translucent rock, where the ultraviolet cannot reach them but visible light still can.

Radiation from space is the other thing people worry about, and this project measured it in an earlier version. Compare the two.

What it takes to deal with it
Ultraviolet light A dusting of pigment, or a millimetre of anything
Cosmic radiation Nothing, on the timescales that matter. At about 0.3 mGy a day it takes tens of thousands of years to harm a tough organism
Air pressure Physics that cannot be changed. See job 1

The famous dangers are the manageable ones. The one that actually decides whether version 5 works is the one nobody talks about.

What the plant does

Plants already make ultraviolet screening pigments. Anyone who has seen the red flush on a mountain plant, or the deep colour of a high-altitude leaf, has seen it happening. The pigments sit in the outer layers of the leaf and absorb the damaging light before it reaches anything delicate.

Version 5 turns that up. It is the plant’s own pathway, switched harder, so this is an edit rather than a foreign gene.

Two things it does at once

Dark pigments absorb sunlight, so they warm the plant. For most of this design warmth is the enemy, because a warm leaf boils. So pigmentation has to be handled with some care, and this is a genuine tension inside version 5 rather than a free win.

The resolution is probably that the plant wants to be dark below ground and at its base, where warmth helps the roots reach water, and screened but not heat-absorbing at the leaf surface. Whether a plant can be both at once is a real design question and we do not have an answer to it.

What is left unknown

The open question Why it is open
How much screening is enough Martian ultraviolet has been measured, and plant pigments have been measured, but nobody has put the two numbers together for a real leaf
Whether screening costs too much Pigment absorbs the light the plant wanted for photosynthesis as well. A plant that screens perfectly and starves has not been helped
Whether it matters at all when the plant is dried out A dormant, dried plant is far more resistant to ultraviolet than a working one. Version 5’s plant is dormant most of the time, so the real exposure may be much smaller than the worst case

That last point may make this job smaller still. The plant is only vulnerable during the hours it is actually working. Those are the hours around dawn and dusk, when the sun is low and its light has further to travel through what atmosphere there is.
