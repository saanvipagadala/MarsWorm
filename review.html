Would you be willing to look at this design?

This project set out to find an organism that could turn Martian dirt into soil. No such organism exists. So the project designed one instead: 37 real genes, each borrowed from an organism that already does that one part of the job, assembled into a single soil bacterium.

Nothing has been built yet. Every part of it can still change. That is exactly why we would like an expert to read the design now.

This is an entry for the 3M Young Scientist Challenge 2027.

Why the project exists

Martian regolith is crushed rock: no organic matter, no nitrogen a plant can use, and roughly 1% perchlorate. A colony has to grow its own food, so that rock has to become soil. And whatever makes it has to reproduce, because you cannot ship enough of anything to Mars.

Making that soil takes two unrelated things at the same time. Doing an earthworm’s work in the rock, which comes to 10 abilities. And surviving Mars while doing it, which takes 13 more. That is 23 abilities in all, and each one is small enough that a single protein could do it.

The obvious first move is to look for an organism that already has all 23 of those abilities. 322 sequenced organisms were scored against the full list, and the best of them managed 14. No organism on Earth has all 23. That is why this project designed one instead of finding one.

The design, in four steps

If no single organism has all 23 abilities, the next question is whether they can be assembled. Each step below is a decision, and each one is a place where the design could be wrong.

The abilities No organism on Earth has all 23. But each ability, taken on its own, is something some organism already has, so the abilities can be borrowed one at a time
The genes Each of the 23 abilities was traced to a real gene in a real organism that already uses it: 37 genes in all, covering both halves of the job, enrichment and survival
The host Genes only work inside a cell. Azotobacter vinelandii is an ordinary soil bacterium that already carries 13 of the 37, so the remaining 24 go in as six cassettes, each tested before the next is ordered
The proof The growth trial puts the finished bacterium into MGS-1 simulant, plants seeds, and finds out whether anything grows

The three questions

An answer to any one of these three would be worth far more to us than general comments on all of them.

What it is about The question we would like answered
1 The host Is Azotobacter vinelandii the right bacterium to build on?
2 The switch Will the nifH promoter drive cassettes that have nothing to do with nitrogen?
3 The experiment Is the growth trial a fair test of the design?

Question 1: is Azotobacter vinelandii the right host?

Why it was chosen A. vinelandii already carries 13 of the 37 genes, fixes nitrogen without a host plant, forms desiccation-resistant cysts, takes up DNA naturally and integrates it by homologous recombination, and is BSL-1
What we are least sure of It is a strict aerobe with one of the highest respiration rates known, which is how it keeps oxygen away from its nitrogenase. One cassette asks it to reduce perchlorate and generate energy anaerobically, which is close to asking it to stop being what it is

Is that conflict a fixable engineering problem, a wrong-organism problem, or a dead end? And if this host is wrong, what would you have picked instead?

Question 2: will the nifH promoter drive these cassettes?

Why it was chosen Every cassette uses A. vinelandii’s own nifH promoter, because it is native, strong, and certain to be recognised in this host
What we are least sure of The nifH promoter is nitrogen-regulated, and most of the six cassettes have nothing to do with nitrogen fixation

Is a nitrogen-regulated promoter a reasonable choice for cassettes like these, or does that regulation make it the wrong switch? What would you use instead?

A cheaper question, and just as useful. The cassettes are 6.0 to 10.1 kb of linear double-stranded DNA with 500 bp homology arms, taken up by natural transformation. Are those arms long enough for reliable integration at that insert size in this organism?

Question 3: is the growth trial a fair test?

Six recipes, three cups each, ten seeds per cup:

What is in the cup Why it is there
1 Simulant only the floor: what Martian dirt gives you unaided
2 Simulant + organic matter controls for the food itself
3 Simulant + organic matter + earthworms the benchmark to beat
4 Simulant + organic matter + the engineered microbe fed the same as the worms, does it do better?
5 Simulant + the microbe, nothing else added the only recipe that could be run on Mars
6 Ordinary potting soil the ceiling: real soil, for scale
Why it is built this way Group 5 is the point of the whole design. On Mars there is no compost to add, so an organism that only works when it is fed has not solved the problem
The second measurement Nitrate is recorded every week, and the week-4 reading is the one compared. A worm can redistribute nitrogen but cannot create any, so nitrate appearing in group 5, where nothing was added, would have had to come from the air
The verdict rule Fixed before any data exists: the worst cup of one group must beat the best cup of the other, by at least 0.10 g

Is a control missing? Is three cups per group defensible? And is week-4 nitrate sound evidence that nitrogen was fixed rather than redistributed?

Two things to know first

Nothing has been built or run The design and the gene selection are finished. The experiments are written up and waiting. Any numbers you find are simulated on purpose, to test the analysis before there is real data to analyse, and every page carrying them says so
It is written in plain language This is a student project, so the pages are written to be readable by one. The reasoning underneath is meant to survive an expert reading anyway, and if the plain wording is hiding a mistake, that is exactly what we hope someone points out
