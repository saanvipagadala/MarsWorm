MarsWorm

Designing an organism that could do an earthworm’s job on Mars. 3M Young Scientist Challenge 2027 · Saanvi

We can get to Mars. We can’t stay.

Getting there is a rocket problem, and rocket problems are mostly solved. Staying is a food problem, and it is not.

A crew eats roughly half a ton of food per person per year, and launch windows to Mars open only about every 26 months. Carry three years of meals and you have spent the whole payload on lunch. Any colony that lasts has to grow food on Mars, and growing food needs soil.

That is where the plan breaks.

Mars has dirt. Mars does not have soil.

Martian regolith Earth soil
Crushed rock and mineral grains ✅ ✅
Dead organic matter ❌ none ✅
Living microbes doing chemistry ❌ none ✅ billions per gram
Nutrients a plant can absorb ❌ locked in rock ✅
Toxic perchlorate salts ✅ ~0.5–1% by weight ❌

Regolith is crushed rock. Soil is crushed rock that has been made alive. Plant a seed in regolith and nothing happens: the nitrogen is absent or locked away, and the perchlorate is poisonous.

So the real task is not farming on Mars. It is manufacturing soil on Mars, with something that copies itself, because shipping soil from Earth is the food problem again wearing a different hat.

On Earth, something already does this job

Earthworms. Feed one dead leaves and it returns castings: dark, crumbly stuff that plants grow in. Except the worm is not really doing the chemistry. Its gut microbes are. The worm is a mobile, burrowing delivery vehicle for them, which is the first clue that the answer may not need to be a worm.

Send one to Mars and it dies before the airlock finishes cycling: −60 °C freezes it, the absent water dries it out (it breathes through moist skin), the radiation shreds its DNA, the perchlorate poisons it, and there is nothing to eat.

We need something that does an earthworm’s job while surviving Mars’s conditions. Nothing on Earth does both.

So we went looking, and then we designed one

322 organisms scored on both jobs at once, and the corner stays empty. The best total is Arabidopsis thaliana at 14 of 23, and it is a plant that would freeze solid before it did anything. No organism gets past 8 of 13 on survival or 7 of 10 on soil enrichment, and none manages a strong score on both: Deinococcus radiodurans shrugs off radiation and scores a flat zero on enrichment.

The organism cannot be found, so it has to be assembled. Every piece of the problem is solved by something: antifreeze proteins in fish, radiation repair in Deinococcus, nitrogen fixation in Azotobacter. Just never by the same something. Take the best real gene for each piece, write them up as an order a DNA synthesis company could print, then test the two halves of the job for real, and build it.

The five stages

Each stage is a folder under phases/, and owns everything it needs: its writeup, its code, its data, its results. The number is there to keep them in order; the name is what it does.

Stage What it answers Status
1 Searching Earth for MarsWorm Does something on Earth already do this job? ✅ done. No: 322 organisms scored; the best reaches 14 of 23, and nothing comes near full marks on both halves
2 Designing MarsWorm If none exists, which real genes would build one? ✅ done. 37 genes, all 23 jobs, 18 source organisms, 41,445 base pairs, roughly $2,900–6,200 to have printed
3 Testing Biology Enriches Dirt Can earthworms turn Mars regolith into something a seedling grows in? protocol + datasheets and analysis pipeline, dry-run on 4 scenarios; experiment not yet run
4 Testing Genes Enable Survival Does drying-protection help survive UV and freezing, and is one gene the reason? protocol + datasheets and analysis pipeline, dry-run on 3 scenarios; experiment not yet run
5 Building MarsWorm What turns a gene list into an organism? ✅ the design is settled: one host, 24 genes, six tested rounds, and five costed ways a lab could build it
6 Testing MarsWorm in Dirt Does the thing we built actually turn Mars dirt into soil? protocol + datasheets and analysis pipeline, dry-run on 4 scenarios; needs an organism nobody has built yet

Stage 1 finds the gap, Stage 2 designs something to fill it, and the design is then scored again by Stage 1’s own rule and put back on the same map. Flip Show our design and watch the empty corner fill. Stages 3 and 4 test the two halves, soil enrichment and survival, in the only way a laptop cannot: with live organisms.

How the gene search works

traits.json  ->  search UniProt  ->  trace/*.md  ->  genes_to_order.md + .csv

One file is written by a human. traits.json holds 13 traits, split into 23 jobs, with 45 search terms. Everything after that comes from the database.

Those terms are not the traits’ own names, and cannot be: searching UniProt for toxic returns snake venom, and iron returns photosystems, which contain iron but do not free it from rock. Plain words name the problem; the terms name the helper.

Nothing limits which organism a gene may come from. Genes are ordered as printed DNA, so the source never has to be grown or shipped. That is why the best antifreeze protein in the order comes from a winter flounder.

A new stage is a new folder. Nothing at the top level grows.

traits.json and the downloaded proteins live in Stage 1, because that is the stage that defines them; Stage 2 reads them from there rather than keeping a second copy.

Known gaps

Written up properly in Stage 2 and scoped in Stage 5.

Two of them are now closed. The host is chosen: Azotobacter vinelandii, which donates 13 of the 37 genes and never has to receive them. With it, the oxygen conflict has a candidate answer, because that host already fixes nitrogen while breathing air. See From 37 genes to MarsWorm.

Still open: no promoters or assembly plan; 34% of the order is one trait; and the oxygen answer is a hypothesis nobody has tested. It is Step 5 of the six-round build for exactly that reason.
