Job 2: unlock the rock

A plant in Martian dirt can starve while standing in its own food. The phosphorus and the iron are both there. They are locked inside rock, and this page is about how a root gets them out.

End to end · ← Job 1 · Job 3 →

What Martian dirt has, and what a plant can reach

Martian dirt is crushed volcanic rock, and volcanic rock is not poor. It holds most of what a plant needs.

Nutrient Is it there Can a root take it
Phosphorus Yes, plenty No. It is bound up in a hard mineral called apatite
Iron Yes, the planet is red with it No. Rust is the wrong form. A root cannot absorb it
Potassium, magnesium, calcium, sulphur Yes Mostly yes
Nitrogen Some, as nitrate Yes, and it is the one that runs out

So two of the three nutrients a plant needs most are present and unreachable. This is not a Martian oddity. Large areas of Earth have the same problem. So the answer already exists.

How plants already solve this on Earth

Roots do chemistry. A root is not a straw. It pushes substances out into the ground around it to change that ground, and then takes back what comes loose.

What the root pushes out What it does
Citric acid and similar weak acids Dissolves rock and releases the phosphorus held in it
Acid, as bare protons Lowers the acidity around the root, which loosens many minerals at once
Iron-grabbing molecules Latch on to iron in rust and carry it back

Plants that live on difficult ground do more of this than plants that do not. Some crops have been bred for it deliberately, to cope with acid soils, and there are crop lines growing on Earth today carrying stronger versions of exactly these traits.

What version 4 changes

Nothing new is invented. The plant’s own rock-dissolving machinery is turned up, and the parts that handle iron are turned up with it.

Because these are the plant’s own genes turned up rather than foreign genes put in, most of this job can be done by editing rather than by adding.

What it costs the plant

Acid is not free. Every molecule of citric acid a root pushes into the ground is carbon the plant built with sunlight and then gave away. A plant that spends heavily on mining grows more slowly than one that does not.

On Earth that trade is usually worth it only in bad ground. Martian dirt is bad ground, so it should be worth it there. Nobody has measured the exchange rate in Martian material. The bench experiments are there to find it.

Why this replaces the worms

In version 3, freeing the phosphorus was a job for a bacterium and then for earthworms chewing through the dirt. In version 4 the plant does it, at the root, in the same motion as growing.

Version 3 Version 4
Who frees the phosphorus A bacterium, then worms, over weeks The root, while growing
What has to be there first A culture, food for it, and time Nothing
What it costs Hydrogen and waiting Some of the plant’s own sugar

What this job cannot fix

It does not make more nutrients. It only unlocks the ones that are there. Phosphorus and iron are abundant enough in Martian rock that unlocking is enough. Nitrogen is not. There is a fixed amount of nitrate in the ground and no root chemistry creates more of it.

So job 2 makes the dirt usable, and nitrogen is still the thing that decides how many harvests come out of the same ground.
