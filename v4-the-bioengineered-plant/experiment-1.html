Experiment 1: is the plant poisoning itself?

Version 4 claims that Martian dirt does not simply poison a plant. It claims the plant makes the poison itself, out of the harmless chlorate in the dirt, using its own nitrogen enzyme. What it makes then destroys that enzyme. Everything else in the design follows from that claim. This experiment is first because it is the cheapest one that can prove us wrong.

What we would be asking of you
The question Does chlorate harm a radish by way of its nitrate reductase, and does handing the plant ammonium instead of nitrate rescue it?
Bench space 16 pots on a plant bench under ordinary lights
How long Four weeks
Biosafety BSL-1. No organisms at all, plus a few grams of chlorate and perchlorate, which are oxidisers
From the lab A plant bench with lights, a drying oven, a scale to 0.001 g, and somewhere to lock two small bottles
From us Everything else, listed below
Student on site A short session a week

Why this one first

What we are looking at What it says
What is known Chlorate is a known substrate of plant nitrate reductase. The chlorite it makes is known to inhibit that enzyme irreversibly. Both have been in the literature for decades
Why the gap matters We cannot find anyone who has joined those two facts to Martian regolith and said this is why crops die in it. If somebody has, we want to cite them rather than claim it
What this decides Whether version 4 has a mechanism or a guess. If ammonium does not rescue the plant, our explanation is wrong and the design needs rewriting

Design

Four treatments, four pots each, 16 pots.

Pot Nitrogen given Poison added
A nitrate chlorate
B nitrate none
C ammonium chlorate
D nitrate perchlorate

The four pots tell one story. Pot B is a radish in clean simulant, so it shows what this dirt can do when nothing is wrong with it. Pot A adds chlorate at the strength Phoenix measured on Mars, about half a percent of the dry weight, which makes it Martian dirt as it really is. If our claim is right, pot A should be sick and pot B should not.

Pot C is where the experiment is won or lost. It gets exactly the same chlorate as pot A, but its nitrogen arrives as ammonium, and a plant uses ammonium without the enzyme we say the chlorite destroys. So pot C is pot A with the damage routed around. If pot C recovers, the mechanism is right. If it stays as sick as pot A, the chlorate is harming the plant some other way, and version 4 needs rewriting.

Pot D asks a smaller question on the side. It swaps chlorate for perchlorate, which we expect to be far less harmful to the plant, because the plant’s own enzyme largely ignores it. Perchlorate is still a problem for whoever eats the radish, and experiment 4 is about that.

What How much

On the chemicals. Chlorate and perchlorate are oxidisers, not poisons in the dramatic sense, but they should be kept away from anything that burns and handled by somebody who normally handles laboratory chemicals. The quantities here are a few grams.

Setup

On day one Why
1 Weigh the same mass of simulant into every pot
2 Dissolve each salt in water first, then stir it evenly through the simulant for its group nothing goes in dry and uneven
3 Water every pot with the nitrogen-free solution, then add nitrate to A, B and D, and the same amount of nitrogen as ammonium to C the nitrogen amount is matched, only its form differs
4 Sow the same number of seeds in every pot, and thin to the same number after emergence
5 Randomise the pots on the bench and keep the key aside

What happens, and when

When What to do
Day 0 Sow, water, and record the starting weight of each pot
Weekly, four times Count the plants, measure leaf area, note colour and any damage. Water to the same weight
Day 28 Harvest everything. Dry and weigh roots and leaves separately. Run the nitrate reductase assay, and measure the nitrate left in the tissue

What we expect to see

Where to look What we expect
Pot A Small, pale plants. Nitrate reductase activity low. Nitrate building up in the tissue, because the plant took it in and could not use it
Pot B Healthy plants. This is what the simulant can do when it is not poisoned
Pot C Close to pot B. This is the result the design predicts, and the only one that confirms the mechanism
Pot D Between A and B, closer to B
What would end it Pot C as sick as pot A. That would mean the damage does not run through nitrate reductase, our explanation is wrong, and the gene we propose would not help

Recording rules

  • Label by number and keep the key aside until every measurement is done.
  • Write down what is seen, not what it means. A pot that dries out, gets knocked over, or germinates badly gets a note on the day, and the note stays in the report whatever it does to the result.

The sheets, blank and filled in

Spreadsheets, printed and filled in by hand at the bench, then typed up exactly as written. Every column is defined in the codebook. They are empty, because nobody has run this yet.

The sheet What goes in it
pots.csv which pot is which treatment, what went into it, and where it sits on the bench
growth.csv the weekly count, leaf area and colour, pot by pot
tissue.csv the harvest. Dry weights, the nitrogen enzyme reading, and the nitrate left in the leaves. The sheet the answer comes from
care_log.csv the weekly watering, and anything that went wrong that day

The same sheets also exist with invented numbers in them, made by a script, so the sheets can be checked before there is real data. This set is the expected outcome: the poisoned pots are sick, and the ones fed ammonium recover. pots · growth · tissue · care_log · a dry run, not a result

The analysis reads those sheets and writes a report: charts, a verdict for each question, and its own checks on the data. The rules that decide the verdicts were fixed before any data existed. See what that report looks like, built from the invented sheets, and it says dry run in its own title
