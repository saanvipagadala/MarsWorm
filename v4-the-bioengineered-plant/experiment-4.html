Experiment 4: where does the perchlorate end up?

A plant that grows beautifully in Martian dirt and concentrates perchlorate in the part people eat is worse than no plant at all. Perchlorate blocks the thyroid from taking up iodine. That is why it is regulated in drinking water on Earth. This experiment is the one version 4 cannot be published without.

What we would be asking of you
The question When a radish grows in perchlorate-bearing Martian simulant, how much perchlorate ends up in the root somebody would eat, and how much in the leaves?
Bench space 16 pots on a plant bench
How long Four weeks
Biosafety BSL-1. No organisms, plus a few grams of perchlorate
From the lab A plant bench, a drying oven, a scale, and perchlorate measurement in plant tissue
From us Everything else
Student on site A short session a week

Why this one is not optional

What we are looking at What it says
What is known Plants take perchlorate up readily, which is why it turns up in lettuce and in milk on Earth wherever groundwater carries it. Leafy plants concentrate it
Why the gap matters Every measurement we can find is from Earth soils at trace levels. Martian dirt holds roughly half a percent by weight, which is thousands of times more
What this decides Whether the cleaning genes have to sit in the root, and whether root-only expression is enough. It also decides whether a radish is the right crop, because a radish root is the part you eat

Design

The whole experiment is one ladder of perchlorate doses, climbed by sixteen pots, four on every rung. The rungs are none at all, then a tenth of Martian strength, then half, then the full amount Phoenix measured, which is about half a percent of the dirt by weight.

Nothing is added to help. No genes, no bacteria, no cleaning of any kind. That says what an ordinary plant does with Martian perchlorate when it is left to get on with it.

Every plant is cut in two before it is weighed. The leaves go one way, the root goes the other, and each is measured for perchlorate on its own. That says where the perchlorate actually ends up.

The root is the half that decides it. A radish root is the part somebody eats, so a plant that sends its perchlorate up into the leaves and keeps its root clean is a very different proposition from one that does the opposite.

Setup

Why
1 Weigh the same simulant into every pot
2 Dissolve the perchlorate and stir it evenly through each group’s simulant nothing goes in dry and uneven
3 Sow, thin to the same number of plants per pot
4 Randomise on the bench and keep the key aside

What happens, and when

When What to do
Weekly Leaf area, plant count, colour. Water to the same weight
Day 28 Harvest. Separate root from leaf before drying. Dry, weigh, and measure perchlorate in each part
Also Measure the perchlorate left in the soil, so the sums can be checked

What we expect to see

Where to look What we expect
Leaves The higher concentration. This is what Earth measurements suggest, and leafy crops are the ones that carry the warnings
Root Lower, but the number that matters for a radish
The verdict Whether perchlorate in the eaten part stays under a stated ceiling. We write that ceiling down before the experiment runs, from the drinking water limits, and we do not move it afterwards
What would end it, or redirect it Perchlorate concentrating in the root at Martian doses. Then a radish is the wrong crop, and version 4 should be designed around a fruit or a grain instead, where the eaten part is furthest from the soil

What this tells the design

If the answer is Then
Both parts stay clean Root-only expression of the cleaning genes is enough, and version 4 stands
The leaves load up, the root stays clean Fine for a radish, and a warning for any leafy crop
The root loads up The crop has to change, and the perchlorate half of job 1 becomes essential rather than optional

Recording rules

  • Label by number and keep the key aside until every measurement is done.
  • Write down what is seen, not what it means.
  • Nothing from this experiment is eaten by anyone, ever.

The sheets, blank and filled in

Spreadsheets, printed and filled in by hand at the bench, then typed up exactly as written. Every column is defined in the codebook. They are empty, because nobody has run this yet.

The sheet What goes in it
pots.csv which pot got which dose of perchlorate
growth.csv the weekly count, leaf area and colour
harvest.csv root and leaf weighed and measured separately. This is the split the whole experiment is for
care_log.csv the weekly watering

The same sheets also exist with invented numbers in them, made by a script, so the sheets can be checked before there is real data. This set is the expected outcome: the leaf clears the food-safety ceiling and the root does not, which is the outcome that would change the crop. pots · growth · harvest · care_log · a dry run, not a result

The analysis reads those sheets and writes a report: charts, a verdict for each question, and its own checks on the data. The rules that decide the verdicts were fixed before any data existed. See what that report looks like, built from the invented sheets, and it says dry run in its own title
