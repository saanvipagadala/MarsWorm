Would your lab have room for two experiments?

This project has spent a year on one question: how would you make soil on Mars? It has got as far as a laptop and a public database can take it, and two of the answers now have to be measured rather than looked up.

Both experiments are finished on paper. The protocols are written, the blank data sheets exist, and the analysis has already been rehearsed against invented numbers, so the only thing still missing is somewhere to run them. This is an entry for the 3M Young Scientist Challenge 2027.

The most important thing first: neither experiment involves any genetic modification. One is earthworms in Martian soil simulant, in cups on a shelf. The other compares normal roundworms with a strain ordered ready-made from a stock centre. BSL-1 throughout. Nothing is engineered, transformed, or built.

Why these two experiments, and not others. A Mars colony has to grow its own food. Martian regolith is not soil. So this project designed a microbe to turn one into the other.

That design assumes two things. First, that life can enrich dead dirt at all. Second, that an ability travels with the gene behind it. Neither has ever been checked. These two experiments check them.

Experiment 1: can earthworms turn Martian dirt into soil?

The assumption That adding life to dead dirt turns it into soil. Nobody has shown that it does
What is set up 15 cups, five recipes, three cups each: bare MGS-1 simulant; simulant and food; simulant, food and worms; simulant, food and worm castings with no live worm; and ordinary potting soil
What happens For four weeks the dirt itself is measured every week. Then radish seeds go into every cup, and three more weeks of growing
What decides it The dried weight of the seedlings on day 21, to 0.01 g. If the worst worm cup still weighs more than the best no-worm cup, and by at least a tenth of the scale, the worms made the soil. If the two overlap, the report says so

Experiment 2: do genes carry abilities?

The assumption That a gene brings its ability with it when it is moved. The design takes 37 genes from 18 organisms and counts on exactly that
What is set up Two strains of C. elegans, one normal and one with a drying gene broken. It is tested backwards: rather than adding a gene to something that lacks an ability, take an animal that has the ability and break the gene behind it
What happens Half the worms of each strain are dried and half are kept damp. Every group then meets UV, freezing or Martian dust, and the survivors are counted
What decides it In the normal strain the dried worms should survive better. If that advantage disappears in the strain missing the gene, the ability really does travel with the gene. Ten percentage points is about five worms on a plate of fifty, and below that, three runs prove nothing
