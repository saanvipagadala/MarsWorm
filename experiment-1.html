Experiment 1: can earthworms turn Martian dirt into soil?

A whole project about growing food on Mars rests on one thing nobody has checked: that putting life into dead Martian dirt turns it into soil at all. This experiment checks it, with earthworms, in fifteen cups on a shelf.

This is the run sheet: the experiment written out exactly as it would be done at your bench, so a lab can see all of it, including the costs, before agreeing to host anything. The reasoning behind every choice in it is in Stage 3.

What we would be asking of you
The question Do earthworms turn MGS-1 Martian regolith simulant into something a seedling will grow in? And if they do, does the earthworm have to be alive in the cup, or do its castings do the same job on their own?
Bench space 15 cups on about half a metre of shelf
How long Seven weeks, start to finish
Biosafety BSL-1. No genetic modification, no vertebrates
From the lab Shelf space, a scale, a drying oven, a pH meter or strips, and a light
From us Everything else, listed below
Student on site Short measuring sessions, a couple of days a week

Design

Only one of the five recipes asks the question directly. Each of the other four rules out a rival explanation, so that anything the worms achieve cannot be credited to something else in the cup.

Three identical cups of each recipe, 15 cups in all, so no single lucky cup can decide anything.

Group What is in the cup Why it is here
1 Regolith only MGS-1 simulant, nothing added the floor: what Mars gives you
2 Regolith + organics MGS-1 + pre-composted leaves and scraps, no worms controls for the food itself: does the organic matter alone do it?
3 Regolith + organics + worms MGS-1 + organics + 3 Eisenia fetida the real thing: live worms doing the work
4 Regolith + organics + castings MGS-1 + organics + harvested castings, no live worms worm poop with no worm in it: is the living worm needed at all?
5 Potting soil ordinary potting soil the ceiling: real soil, for scale

Groups 2 and 4 are what make this an experiment rather than a demonstration. Group 2 asks whether the food alone does the work. Group 4 asks whether the living worm is needed at all.

The castings for group 4 are made beforehand. A separate small bin of ordinary soil and food scraps runs for about two weeks. The dark crumbly castings are then harvested and the worms picked back out.

Cup size is set by the worms, not the plants. The cups are 8 oz cups holding about 200 mL, with drainage holes. Below roughly 100 mL a red wiggler cannot burrow, and the cup dries out within a day under a grow light.

Order 4 kg of simulant. Twelve of the fifteen cups use it, which comes to about 3.1 kg. The spare kilogram is deliberate. If the worms die in week 1 or a batch is spoiled, it is the difference between rebuilding on the weekend and waiting on a delivery.

Setup

Everything starts on one afternoon. The cups are filled to their recipes, a cotton square goes into each, and then they are set out on the shelf in a particular order that matters more than it looks.

On day one Why
1 Build the 15 cups to the recipes above
2 Bury one cotton square in each cup it gets dug up in weeks 2, 3 and 4, and how much has rotted away is the measure of how much life is in that cup
3 Lay the cups out in the shuffled bench_position order from containers.csv, never grouped by recipe lined up by recipe, one group quietly gets the brightest, warmest end of the shelf, and nothing done later can undo that

Then keep everything else identical. Same room, same light schedule of 12 to 14 hours a day, same watering, for all fifteen cups.

Phase 1, weeks 1 to 4: watching the dirt change

For four weeks nothing is planted. The worms work, and the dirt is measured around them, because the question is whether it is turning into soil before anything is asked to grow in it.

Six readings, once a week, from every cup, written straight into data/weekly_measurements.csv.

The measurement How
1 Crumbs dry a 10 g scoop, tip onto the 2 mm sieve, shake, weigh what stays on top
2 Water held weigh that scoop dry, water until it stops dripping, weigh again
3 pH strip, in soil stirred into water
4 Nitrate aquarium strip, same cup
5 Cloth decay dig up one buried square in weeks 2, 3 and 4, then rinse, dry, weigh
6 Photo from directly above, same distance and lighting, coded by number

Return each scoop to the cup it came from once it has been weighed. The cups are small. Four weeks of sampling would otherwise empty them.

Every line above is one reading or one weighing. Nothing is averaged or converted at the bench.

Phase 2, weeks 4 to 7: finding out whether anything grows

Four weeks of measuring the dirt says whether it looks more like soil. Planting says whether it is. Every cup gets the same ten seeds on the same day, and three weeks later the answer is a number on a scale.

When What to do
1 Week 4 Sow 10 seeds of one species in every cup, all from the same packet
2 Every day Water identically, and log every watering
3 Every 2 to 3 days Count the sprouts that are up, and measure their heights with a ruler
4 Day 21 Cut the sprouts at soil level, dry them, and weigh them. This is the headline number

The seeds all come from one packet on purpose. Seed batches germinate differently, and using two would let the seeds win the experiment instead of the dirt.

Recording rules: what goes on the page, and what does not

Four rules govern everything anyone writes down. Each one closes a way the experiment could quietly mislead whoever reads it later.

The rule What goes wrong without it
Write down only what an instrument reads a sheet saying “held 34% water” can never be checked, because the weights behind it are gone
A blank cell means not measured. Never type 0 a 0 is a measurement, and it says nothing grew. That is a different claim, and the analysis treats the two differently
All 15 cups measured on the same day a cup measured three days later has had three more days to change
Report all three cups of a group, never just the average an average hides the one cup that disagrees

The sheets, blank and filled in

Four spreadsheets hold this experiment, printed and filled in by hand at the bench, then typed up exactly as written. Every column in them is defined in the codebook. They are empty, because nobody has run this yet.

The sheet What goes in it
containers.csv which cup is which recipe, and where it sits on the shelf
weekly_measurements.csv the dirt, once a week for four weeks
plant_measurements.csv sprout counts and heights, every 2 to 3 days
harvest.csv day 21, the dried seedlings, weighed

The same four sheets also exist with numbers in them, so you can see what a finished run looks like before agreeing to produce one. Every number in them was invented by a script, written to test the analysis before there is any real data to analyse. This set is the hoped-for outcome, where the worms make a difference.

What the sheets turn into

The analysis reads those four sheets and writes a report: charts, a verdict for each question, and its own checks on the data. The rules that decide the verdicts were fixed before any data existed, so a disappointing result prints exactly as plainly as a good one.

Waste, and what happens to the worms

Compost and potting soil ordinary waste
The worms rehomed to a garden or a compost bin. Eisenia fetida is a commercially sold composting organism, and is not released into wild habitat
